How Our Relationship Tests Work

A transparent explanation of how relationship.sbs turns scenario-based answers into educational result profiles.

Our tests are designed for self-reflection. They help people notice recurring patterns in communication, closeness, conflict, boundaries, emotional availability, texting, and relationship safety.

What these tests are not

  • They are not diagnostic instruments.
  • They do not determine whether another person has a mental-health condition.
  • They are not substitutes for therapy, medical care, legal advice, or emergency support.
  • They should not be used as the sole basis for a major safety or relationship decision.

1. Question design

Questions are written around concrete relationship situations rather than abstract labels. Examples include changes in texting, repair after conflict, reactions to closeness, boundary-setting, reassurance, follow-through, emotional presence, and repeated control or withdrawal patterns.

Scenario-based wording is used because behavior in context is usually more useful for reflection than asking a person to choose a label for themselves.

Where a topic involves a clinical or diagnostic term, the test is framed around observable relationship patterns instead of diagnosing a person. For example, the Narcissistic Relationship Test examines control, blame-shifting, entitlement, and gaslighting-style confusion without determining whether someone has Narcissistic Personality Disorder.

2. Scoring

Each answer contributes points to one or more predefined response dimensions or result profiles. The exact scoring model depends on the test.

  • Dimension-based tests can combine scores across dimensions. The Attachment Style Test, for example, tracks anxiety and avoidance patterns and uses thresholds to map combinations into a profile.
  • Profile-based tests assign points directly to outcome patterns. Several tests compare which response profile receives the strongest total score.
  • Pattern tags may be used in some tests to identify supporting themes or internal consistency signals.

Because the tests measure different questions, there is no single universal scoring formula across all 14 tests. Scores are used to organize answers into a practical result profile; they are not clinical severity scores.

3. How a result is selected

When you finish a test, the site totals the points attached to your selected answers and applies that test's scoring rules. The result shown is the profile that best matches the pattern represented by those answers.

Some results also include supporting themes, scripts, blind spots, cautions, or next steps. These are educational prompts tied to the result profile, not personalized treatment recommendations.

4. Interpretation

A useful result should help you ask better questions: What repeats? What happens under stress? What changes after a clear request? Is repair mutual? Are boundaries respected? Does the pattern improve over time?

Results should be interpreted alongside real behavior and context. A single difficult week can look very different from a pattern that repeats for months. Likewise, a calm score does not invalidate a concrete safety concern.

5. Safety-sensitive topics

Tests involving manipulation, coercion, toxic patterns, trauma-bond dynamics, or narcissistic-style relationship patterns require extra caution. A score cannot determine whether abuse is occurring or whether another person has a disorder.

If you are experiencing threats, coercion, stalking, violence, severe control, or immediate danger, prioritize safety and qualified local support rather than relying on a test result.

6. Privacy and processing

Test scoring runs in your browser. The test engine reads the answers you select and calculates score totals locally for the result shown on the page.

No account is required to take the tests. If you choose to create or use a share link, the link can contain an encoded summary of the result profile so the result can be reopened or shared. Treat a share link as information you chose to disclose and share it only with people you trust.

For broader site data practices, see the Privacy Policy.

7. Editorial and review principles

  • Observable behavior over labels: questions focus on patterns a person can notice.
  • Plain language: results aim to be understandable without clinical jargon.
  • Non-diagnostic framing: tests describe response patterns rather than diagnosing conditions.
  • Actionable reflection: results include practical next steps, scripts, or questions where appropriate.
  • Safety over certainty: a test score never overrides direct evidence of harm, coercion, or danger.
  • Corrections: wording, scoring, and explanatory content can be revised when an error or ambiguity is identified.

8. Limitations

These tests have important limitations. They rely on self-reported answers, which can be influenced by mood, recent events, memory, interpretation, and the specific relationship you had in mind while answering.

The tests have not been presented on this site as clinically validated diagnostic instruments, and we do not publish sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic accuracy, or population norms for them. A result should therefore be treated as structured self-reflection, not measurement of a medical or psychiatric condition.

9. Updates and corrections

We may update a test when wording is unclear, a scoring path needs correction, safety language can be improved, or supporting educational content needs revision. Material changes should preserve the core intent of the test while improving clarity and usefulness.

If you find a confusing question, a technical problem, or a result that appears inconsistent with the answers shown, email info@relationship.sbs.
