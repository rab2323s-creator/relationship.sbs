Aromantic vs. Asexual: What Is the Difference?

Aromantic vs. asexual: aromantic (aro) usually describes little or no romantic attraction, while asexual (ace) usually describes little or no sexual attraction. They are different orientations, and someone can identify with one, both (aroace), or neither. Neither label tells you by itself whether someone dates, has sex, loves others, or wants a committed partner.

Imagine two questions instead of one. “Do I experience romantic attraction to someone?” is different from “Do I experience sexual attraction to someone?” A person might answer differently to each question. People may also use broader or more specific terms on the aromantic and asexual spectrums. This distinction appears in resources from AUREA, TAAAP, and the Oxford University LGBTQ+ Society.

It is easy to hear aromantic and think “doesn't want a relationship,” or hear asexual and think “doesn't have a sex drive.” Both shortcuts confuse attraction with behavior, preferences, and circumstances. A clearer picture gives you language without turning your life into an exam.

The distinction in four short lines

  • Aromantic: about romantic attraction, not someone's capacity for affection.
  • Asexual: about sexual attraction, not a rule about sexual behavior or libido.
  • Aroace: commonly describes someone on both the aromantic and asexual spectra.
  • Identity is personal: a label can be helpful, optional, provisional, or changed later.

The Two Invitations: An Original Story About Romance, Attraction and Words

Sometimes the most confusing thing is not what you feel. It is discovering that the world expects two different feelings to arrive together—and that you are supposed to recognize both without ever having them explained.

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday. Inside was an invitation to a friend's engagement party, a small pale-green card with two names in gold. Lena held it at the kitchen table, pleased for her friend. But she was startled by the word finally in a message beneath it: “Maybe you'll be next!”

She had never been certain what “next” meant. She could admire the way someone looked, could be drawn to a voice, could want to sit close and share a private joke. But when friends described a crush as the wish to be somebody's boyfriend or girlfriend, the feeling didn't quite match. Sometimes she had tried to want the story because everyone around her spoke as if it were inevitable.

Her friend Sam sat opposite her, stirring tea. Sam understood something different. Sam loved the idea of falling in love and pictured a future partner vividly, but rarely felt sexual attraction to anybody. They had spent years being told this was just shyness or something a perfect date would solve. The two friends were not having the same experience, even though other people had given them the same advice.

Lena turned over the card. “What if I want closeness,” she asked, “but not that particular kind of story?” Sam shrugged gently. “Maybe there are more ways to be close than the invitation describes.” They didn't settle on labels that evening. They wrote down what each of them did want: honesty, companionship, time together, room to say no. The table looked the same afterward. The language didn't.

Fictional editorial story. Lena and Sam are invented composite scenarios to illustrate two distinctions; they are not interviews, research participants, or models for what every aro or ace person feels.

Aromantic Meaning: What Does Being Aromantic Actually Mean?

Aromantic meaning: an aromantic person experiences little to no romantic attraction, or relates to romantic attraction in a way outside familiar expectations. “Aro” is a common abbreviation; “arospec” describes the wider aromantic spectrum. Some aromantic people seek committed partnerships, some enjoy romance-coded activities, and others prefer not to date.

Romantic attraction is sometimes described as a pull toward a particular person as a potential romantic partner, but there is no single sensation everyone agrees defines a crush. Romantic gestures also differ by culture and context. Holding someone's hand, sleeping beside them, or liking a love song is not a reliable measure of orientation.

According to AUREA's community-informed FAQ, aromantic people can have friendships, chosen family, queerplatonic bonds, and—if they choose—romantic partnerships. The term is not shorthand for coldness, emotional unavailability, commitment avoidance, or being unable to care.

Does aromantic mean you cannot fall in love?

Many aromantic people do not experience romantic love; others on the spectrum may experience it rarely or under certain circumstances. Love itself can refer to deep friendship, care, family bonds, partnership, and more. The better question is what kind of attraction or relationship this person experiences, rather than forcing one definition of love on everyone.

Is being aromantic the same as choosing to be single?

No. Single is a relationship status. Aromantic describes an orientation or a place on a spectrum of romantic attraction. An alloromantic person can choose to be single. An aromantic person can be partnered, married, nonpartnering, or undecided. None of those choices invalidates a label.

Asexual Meaning: Why Sexual Attraction Is Not the Same as Sex Drive

Asexual meaning: an asexual person generally experiences little or no sexual attraction to others. Asexuality is an orientation, not the same as celibacy, low libido, abstinence, inexperience, or a medical diagnosis. Some asexual people date, fall in love, masturbate, have sex, or have children; others do not.

Sexual attraction is attraction directed toward a person. Libido is a general drive or interest in sexual experience, which does not always need a particular person as its focus. Behavior is what a person chooses to do. Those three things can differ. The Oxford University LGBTQ+ Society explicitly distinguishes orientation from sex drive, and PFLAG distinguishes asexuality from voluntary abstinence.

Attraction“Do I experience a sexual pull toward this person?”
Libido“Do I feel sexual desire or arousal in general?”
Choice“What am I comfortable doing, if anything?”
Consent“Is this freely chosen, specific, and reversible?”

A person who has sex is not automatically allosexual. A person who avoids sex is not automatically asexual. Some people have an asexual identity and welcome partnered sex; others feel indifferent or repulsed by it. None owes sex to prove affection, and no partner owes a specific relationship structure. Compatibility requires honest, pressure-free discussion.

When desire or pleasure changes suddenly, feels distressing, or comes with pain or other symptoms, someone may choose to talk to a qualified healthcare professional. That is about the change or distress, not about correcting asexuality: an ace identity itself is not a disorder. For a separate relationship-specific discussion of desire, see our desire and intimacy reflection quiz, which cannot determine anyone's orientation.

Aromantic vs. Asexual vs. Aroace: The Key Differences at a Glance

Aromantic, asexual, and aroace are not synonyms. Aromantic refers primarily to romantic attraction; asexual refers primarily to sexual attraction; aroace refers to someone who identifies on both spectra. Alloromantic means not on the aromantic spectrum, while allosexual means not on the asexual spectrum.

Romantic vs. Sexual Attraction: Our Five-Layer Relationship Lens

Romantic attraction and sexual attraction are two distinct questions, not an entire relationship. Our Five-Layer Relationship Lens separates attraction, desire, behavior, relationship hopes, and agreements so that people can talk clearly without guessing a label from a single experience.

This is an original editorial reflection framework developed by relationship.sbs, informed by distinctions in community education resources—not a psychological assessment, a clinical theory, or a validated way to identify orientation. It is meant to clarify what a sentence is actually saying.

  1. Attraction: Who, if anyone, pulls your attention? Romantic and sexual attraction may line up, differ, appear rarely, or be difficult to distinguish. A specific person might matter; a particular activity might not.
  2. Desire: What do you feel like wanting? Wanting physical release, touch, affection, or companionship does not automatically mean attraction of a particular kind. This layer is about your own felt wants.
  3. Behavior: What do you do or choose not to do? Going on dates, kissing, having sex, declining a date, or being single are actions and circumstances. They do not independently prove orientation.
  4. Relationship hopes: What kind of life together, if any, appeals to you? Some want a romantic partnership, a queerplatonic bond, a close friendship network, a shared home, or independence. These hopes can evolve.
  5. Agreements: What is welcome, freely chosen and negotiated? Consent, boundaries, privacy, commitment and communication make relationships workable regardless of labels.

Example: “I don't want sex tonight” belongs to the behavior/consent conversation. It does not tell you whether the speaker is asexual. “I have never experienced romantic attraction” is an attraction statement. “I want to build a home with you” expresses a relationship hope. Three different questions can coexist.

The split-attraction model can help some people describe different romantic and sexual orientations; other people do not find separating attraction useful. Our lens is optional in the same spirit. Use what helps you speak more honestly, and discard what doesn't.

Aroace Meaning: Can You Be Both Aromantic and Asexual?

Aroace meaning: “aroace” commonly refers to someone who identifies on both the aromantic and asexual spectrums. It does not mean having no emotions, no friendships, no intimacy, or no interest in partnership. Some aroace people prefer being single; others create committed, non-romantic partnerships or other chosen forms of closeness.

An aroace person may have a deep bond with a friend, enjoy affectionate touch, raise children, live with a partner, build chosen family or prefer to live independently. Those are life arrangements rather than tests of orientation. “Aroace” does not describe one uniform level of comfort with sex, romance, touch or labels.

AUREA's guidance on relationships and romantic attraction emphasizes that aromantic identity doesn't preclude partnership. The University of California, Santa Barbara's ace/aro resource center rejects the idea that either identity removes a person's ability to have meaningful relationships.

What does alloromantic asexual mean?

Someone who identifies as alloromantic and asexual experiences romantic attraction but little or no sexual attraction. They may want romance, commitment or dating while making their own decisions about sex. The orientation does not dictate whether a relationship is exclusive, nonsexual, long-term or casual.

What does aromantic allosexual mean?

Someone who identifies as aromantic and allosexual experiences sexual attraction while experiencing little or no romantic attraction. They may or may not want partnered sex or commitment. Sexual attraction does not obligate someone to date romantically, and not wanting romance does not imply treating partners without care.

Aromantic Spectrum: Greyromantic and Demiromantic Meaning

The aromantic spectrum includes people who experience no romantic attraction and people whose romantic attraction is rare, limited, conditional, or otherwise different from common expectations. Greyromantic often means experiencing romantic attraction rarely or weakly; demiromantic often means attraction can develop only after a strong emotional bond.

Greyromantic meaning: What does grayromantic mean?

Greyromantic (also spelled grayromantic, grey-romantic or gray-aromantic) is a word some people use when their romantic attraction is infrequent, faint, hard to place, or occurs in particular circumstances. It does not require counting exactly how many crushes you have had. “Rare” has no universal numeric threshold. Someone may find the word useful for years, or may eventually choose another description.

Demiromantic meaning: Is it just wanting to know someone first?

Demiromantic usually describes someone who does not experience romantic attraction until a significant emotional bond is established. This differs from choosing to date slowly for trust or safety. A person who feels immediate romantic attraction but waits months before acting on it is not necessarily demiromantic. Importantly, a close bond can be a prerequisite without guaranteeing that attraction will happen.

Demisexual vs. demiromantic: What is the difference?

Demisexual concerns sexual attraction arising only after a close emotional bond; demiromantic concerns romantic attraction arising under that condition. A person can use either term, both, or neither. TAAAP's terminology guide is explicit that emotional closeness is necessary for certain demi experiences but does not guarantee attraction.

Do I need a microlabel?

No. Some find labels such as greyromantic, demiromantic or quoiromantic clarifying. Others simply say “I experience attraction differently,” or choose not to label themselves. Identity words should widen choices, not create another standard people must meet. Consult AUREA's identity terms for community-used definitions, which can develop over time.

Can Aromantic People Have Relationships, Date or Marry?

Yes. Aromantic people may have romantic partnerships, queerplatonic relationships, close friendships, chosen families or no partnerships at all. Aromantic orientation does not predetermine relationship status, commitment, affection or the ability to show care. The useful question is what kinds of closeness the people involved actually choose.

A queerplatonic relationship (often shortened to QPR) is a committed bond that its participants understand as non-romantic even when its importance or structure exceeds conventional expectations of friendship. It can include shared finances, a home, affection, co-parenting or some behaviors others code as romantic. The word does not prescribe sex, exclusivity or living arrangements. AUREA's FAQ and the UCSB glossary explain this diversity.

Can an aromantic person be in love with someone?

Some aromantic-spectrum people experience romantic attraction occasionally or under certain conditions. Others don't experience romantic love but love friends or partners in ways they consider deeply meaningful. Asking an individual what “in love” means to them is more respectful than deciding from the outside.

Can Asexual People Fall in Love, Have Sex or Want a Partner?

Yes. An asexual person can experience romantic attraction and fall in love, date or marry. Some asexual people also choose sex for reasons such as pleasure, intimacy or building a family, while others do not. None of these choices proves or disproves asexuality, and consent remains essential.

An alloromantic asexual person may strongly desire a romantic relationship even without sexual attraction. An aromantic asexual person may not seek romance but still want emotional companionship or a queerplatonic partner. These are examples, not the only possibilities. An ace person may also choose to be single and content with friendship, family or community.

If partners have different sexual preferences, neither person's needs should be dismissed. The conversation is about compatibility and voluntary agreements: what each person wants, which activities are welcome, whether pressure has entered the relationship and whether they can respect a no. A label is not a promise to have sex, and a partner's frustration is not permission to cross boundaries. For a different angle on needs and consent, read our guide to expressing needs clearly.

The Trevor Project's overview discusses the wide variety of relationships and experiences among ace people. It is not appropriate to infer attraction from someone being sexually active—or to assume low libido or avoidance is an ace identity.

Aromantic vs. Asexual Examples: Six Everyday Scenarios

Examples make the difference clearer: someone can want romance without sexual attraction, feel sexual attraction without romance, value partnership without either, or experience a kind of attraction rarely. These six fictional scenarios show how people might describe their experience—not how to classify a stranger.

  1. 1. The person who wants candlelit dates but not sexual attraction

    Imani imagines a romantic partnership: planned dates, affectionate messages, and a shared future. But she seldom feels sexual attraction to anyone. She might find “asexual and alloromantic” useful. Her experience isn't simply being shy on a date.

  2. 2. The person who experiences sexual attraction without a wish for romance

    Leo sometimes feels sexually attracted to someone and still has no interest in a romantic relationship with them. “Aromantic and allosexual” may fit. That does not mean Leo is incapable of ethical, caring commitments.

  3. 3. The person who wants a lifelong companion without romance or sex

    Noor wants to share a home with a trusted friend and plan for each other's wellbeing, but neither romantic nor sexual attraction is central. “Aroace” might fit; “queerplatonic partner” might describe the bond. Neither word is mandatory.

  4. 4. The person who has experienced a romantic crush twice in many years

    Erin rarely recognizes romantic attraction, although it has happened. “Greyromantic” might help describe the pattern. There is no official number of crushes that makes someone qualify.

  5. 5. The person whose romantic attraction appears after friendship

    After a long, emotionally close friendship, Jules notices romantic feelings that were not there before. If romantic attraction only ever develops after a close bond for them, “demiromantic” may resonate. Taking things slowly, by itself, says nothing about orientation.

  6. 6. The person who likes romance fiction but not romance for themselves

    Alex loves novels about people falling in love, but doesn't wish to participate in those plots personally. Enjoying a story does not reveal a person's romantic or sexual orientation. Alex may identify with an aro term—or may simply enjoy fiction.

All six scenarios are invented to illuminate language, not documented biographies, screening criteria, or predictions. People with the same label can have very different lives.

Seven Myths About Aromantic and Asexual People—And What They Miss

The biggest mistake is treating aro or ace as an absence of humanity. Neither identity makes a person incapable of love, emotion, closeness, consent, or lifelong commitment. Attraction, relationship structure and behavior are not interchangeable.

Myth 1: Aromantic means asexual.

Reality: they refer to romantic and sexual attraction separately. An aromantic person can experience sexual attraction; an asexual person can experience romantic attraction.

Myth 2: Asexual means celibate.

Reality: celibacy is a chosen practice or behavior. Asexuality is an orientation; asexual people can make different choices about sex.

Myth 3: Aromantic people cannot love.

Reality: love is not only romance. Friendships, care, family and other bonds can be deeply meaningful. Some people also use aromantic-spectrum labels while occasionally experiencing romance.

Myth 4: An ace or aro person just has attachment issues.

Reality: attachment patterns describe how people navigate closeness and security; aro/ace terms describe experiences of attraction. They can coexist, but one does not explain away the other.

Myth 5: Asexual people have no libido.

Reality: libido and attraction are distinct. Asexual people can experience arousal or desire; others may not.

Myth 6: A close friendship is proof of a hidden crush.

Reality: closeness and romantic attraction are not synonyms. Our best-friend crush guide is about interpreting feelings toward one friend; this article defines distinct orientation terms.

Myth 7: A label must be permanent to be real.

Reality: people may find stable words, evolving words, or no labels helpful. You do not need to produce evidence or promise your future feelings to explain yourself today.

UCSB's myth-and-reality resource and PFLAG's ace/aro page provide clear community education on several of these points.

What Research Actually Shows About Aromanticism and Asexuality

Research supports treating romantic and sexual orientations as distinguishable experiences. Studies also show variation in whether aromantic and asexual people want partnered relationships. But most studies use self-selected samples, and survey responses cannot tell us how any particular person should identify or live.

What a 2025 study found about relationships—not everyone's future

A study by sociologist Hannah Tessler, published in Sexualities, analyzed the 2020 Aromantic Census and 2020 Asexual Community Survey alongside 48 interviews with adults who identified within the aro/ace spectra. It compared experiences and hopes for romantic and other forms of partnered life. In the sampled U.S. single participants, the author found that interest in partnerships differed across aromantic, asexual and combined identities. Read the original study and methods.

The essential limitation: both online survey datasets were convenience samples linked to community networks, not population-representative surveys. They also asked different questions about partnership. We deliberately do not convert these findings into rules like “aromantic people don't want partners” or claims about how common an identity is.

What qualitative research adds about being understood

A 2024 qualitative investigation with the aromantic community explored how participants described identity, stigma, and the experience of finding words for attraction. It highlights the risk of making aromanticism an assumed subset of asexuality rather than studying it in its own right. Qualitative research can illuminate lived experiences; it does not establish prevalence or clinical diagnostic rules. Read the open-access study.

Why we don't attach diagnostic percentages

None of these sources validates a quiz that decides a stranger is “83% aromantic” or uses how often someone has dated to assign a label. Our explanatory tables and private tool below distinguish language, not people. If a self-description helps you, you may use it; if not, curiosity is enough.

Am I Aromantic, Asexual or Neither? Five Questions to Reflect On

No checklist can prove you are aromantic or asexual. If you are curious, notice whether you experience romantic attraction, sexual attraction, both, neither, rarely, or in ways that are hard to distinguish. Then separately ask what kinds of relationships and boundaries you want. You do not have to decide today.

  1. When I think of a crush, what do I actually mean? Do I want romantic partnership with a particular person, physical closeness, friendship, admiration, or something I can't neatly name?
  2. Have I experienced sexual attraction toward particular people? How is that different, if at all, from libido, fantasy, curiosity, or behavior?
  3. Are the feelings frequent, rare, conditional, changeable—or simply unknown? A spectrum can make space for complexity without demanding a score.
  4. If nobody expected me to date or marry, what relationships would I build? Romantic commitment, close friends, queerplatonic partnership, chosen family, or a life without a partner are all legitimate possibilities.
  5. Which words help me communicate, and which make me feel boxed in? You can take a label, modify it, or set it down. No audience gets to vote.

It's also okay to explore other identity words if relevant. If a situation involves one close friend, our friendship-versus-crush article focuses on that narrower experience.

How to Talk to a Partner About Different Romantic and Sexual Needs

Start by describing your experience rather than debating whether your label is correct. Explain what kinds of closeness you enjoy, which actions are optional, and which boundaries are firm. Invite your partner to describe their needs without treating either person as defective. Compatibility is negotiated, never owed.

People can care deeply about each other and want different things from romance or sexual intimacy. That does not automatically mean a relationship is doomed or that love will overcome every mismatch. Begin with an exchange of needs, not promises nobody can keep.

If you think you might be aromantic

“I care about you, and I've been thinking about how I experience romantic attraction. Some familiar romantic expectations don't describe what I feel. I'd like to talk about what closeness, commitment and affection mean to each of us, without rushing toward a label or an answer tonight.”

If you identify as asexual

“I want to share something about how I experience sexual attraction. Being asexual doesn't settle every question about affection, dating or physical intimacy for me. I'd like us to discuss what feels good, what doesn't, and whether our hopes can fit without either person feeling pressured.”

If you are the partner who hears this

“Thank you for trusting me. I don't want to assume what this means for us. Which parts of our relationship feel right to you, and what would you like to change? I want to be honest about my own needs, too.”

If you want a committed non-romantic bond

“I don't use romance as the main language for closeness, but shared plans and loyalty matter to me. Would you want to talk about what a committed friendship or queerplatonic partnership would look like in practice?”

Four agreements to discuss rather than assume

  1. Language: Which words—partner, friend, romantic, queerplatonic—feel comfortable to each of you?
  2. Affection: Which kinds of touch or expression are wanted, unwanted, or negotiable without pressure?
  3. Structure: What do you want from commitment, exclusivity, cohabitation, support or time together?
  4. Revision: How will you revisit agreements if your understanding or needs change?

For help saying something difficult without sounding accusatory, read our communication-needs guide. If boundaries are not being respected, see boundaries vs. control. Orientation should never be used to pressure someone into sex or romance or to shame someone for wanting a different arrangement.

Frequently Asked Questions: Aromantic vs. Asexual

Quick answers: aromantic and asexual refer to different dimensions of attraction; aroace refers to identifying with both. Neither determines libido, sexual behavior, relationship status, or a person's capacity for meaningful connection.

What is the main difference between aromantic and asexual?

Aromantic refers mainly to experiencing little or no romantic attraction. Asexual refers mainly to experiencing little or no sexual attraction. A person may identify with one, both, or neither, and those terms don't prescribe dating or sex.

Can someone be aromantic but not asexual?

Yes. Someone may identify as aromantic while experiencing sexual attraction. Some use the phrase aromantic allosexual. That does not determine whether they want sex, a relationship, or a particular arrangement.

Can you be asexual but not aromantic?

Yes. Some asexual people experience romantic attraction and want romantic relationships. The term alloromantic asexual can describe that combination; it doesn't imply any specific sexual behavior.

What does aroace mean?

Aroace generally means identifying with both the aromantic and asexual spectra. An aroace person may still form deeply caring bonds, have a partner, or prefer being single; experiences differ.

What is the aromantic spectrum?

The aromantic spectrum, often called arospec, includes people with no romantic attraction and people whose romantic attraction is rare, limited, conditional, or otherwise different from common expectations.

What is the difference between greyromantic and demiromantic?

Greyromantic often refers to romantic attraction experienced rarely, weakly or under limited circumstances. Demiromantic typically means romantic attraction develops only after a strong emotional bond—and that bond does not guarantee attraction.

Are asexual and low libido the same thing?

No. Asexuality concerns sexual attraction, while libido is the desire or drive for sexual activity. An asexual person may have a libido and choose whether to act on it.

Can asexual people fall in love?

Yes. Some asexual people experience romantic attraction and love. Others are aromantic or prefer different forms of closeness. Asexuality alone doesn't tell you a person's romantic feelings.

Can aromantic people have a romantic partner?

Yes. Aromantic people can choose romantic or non-romantic partnerships, friendships, queerplatonic bonds, or no partnership. Orientation describes attraction, not the one acceptable way to live.

Is aromanticism the same as fear of commitment or avoidant attachment?

No. Aromanticism describes an orientation; commitment avoidance and attachment patterns describe different experiences. Neither predicts the other. It is harmful to assume someone needs fixing because of an aro identity.

Is aromanticism or asexuality a disorder?

No. Aro and ace identities are not illnesses and do not need to be cured. If someone has a new, distressing change in desire or pain, they can seek support for the symptoms without treating their orientation as a problem.

How do I know if I'm aromantic or asexual?

There isn't a medical test or a guaranteed checklist. Explore how you experience romantic and sexual attraction separately, notice what words help, and talk with supportive people if you choose. You don't owe anyone a definitive label.
