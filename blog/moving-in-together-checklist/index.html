Moving in Together Checklist: 30 Questions to Ask Before You Share a Home

Before moving in together, use a checklist to agree on how you'll share rent, bills, household work, personal space and daily routines—then decide what happens if one person moves out. These 30 questions cover money, lease responsibilities, chores, guests, sleep, pets, conflict and an exit plan. Discuss them before signing, record practical agreements and print the checklist to revisit once you're living together.

The Apartment Looked Perfect. The Expectations Didn't Match.

After six apartment tours, Jules and Avery found a place they loved. The windows caught the afternoon sun. The kitchen had enough space for two people to cook, and the rent was lower than what they paid separately. They imagined a housewarming dinner before they had even applied.

Then the landlord asked whose name would be on the lease. Jules thought they would split everything evenly. Avery earned less and expected an income-based arrangement. Neither had said so. By the time they discussed a sofa, they had also discovered different ideas about overnight guests and where Avery's dog would sleep.

They were not suddenly incompatible. They were encountering ordinary questions that romance had not required them to answer. So they stopped comparing rugs and opened a notebook: one question, both answers, one agreement at a time.

Jules and Avery are fictional characters used to illustrate common planning conversations.

Before Moving In Together: Agree on the Decision, Not Just the Address

Before moving in together, ask what this step means to each of you. One partner may see sharing a home as a long-term commitment; another may see it as a practical experiment. Both views deserve an honest conversation before a lease or mortgage makes the choice expensive to reverse.

The Gottman Institute's guide to questions before cohabiting similarly emphasizes discussing expectations, privacy, everyday habits and shared finances in advance. The useful principle is not that couples must agree on every preference. It is that they should understand their differences before depending on one another for housing.

Try the straightforward test: if rent were equally affordable apart, would you both still choose to move in now? If the answer is uncertain, explore the reason rather than forcing a yes. It is possible to love your partner and still need more time, savings or confidence in conflict resolution.

Money Questions: Budget, Rent, Deposits and the Lease

The most useful moving in together checklist begins with numbers you can verify. List proposed rent, utilities, groceries, internet, insurance, transport changes and likely household supplies. Then add one-time expenses such as movers, deposits and duplicate furniture. A cheaper rent does not automatically make moving affordable when upfront costs are high.

Agree whether bills will be divided equally, proportionally or by another method. Neither a 50/50 split nor an income-based split is universally fair. Compare actual take-home capacity, other obligations and how you want to share nonfinancial household work. Write down who pays what, when it is due and how changes will be handled.

Conversation starter

“Before we apply, can we work through a realistic month? I want both of us to know what we can pay comfortably, including an unexpected expense.”

Read the lease rather than assuming a private agreement between partners changes obligations to a landlord. Screening requirements, deposits and termination rules vary by location. The US Federal Trade Commission's tenant-screening guide explains some US application and screening rights; housing laws elsewhere may be different. Household Responsibilities: Who Does the Work—and Who Notices It?

A chore split sounds easy until the kitchen is messy and one person believes cleaning means “when I notice it” while the other expects dishes before bed. Agree on a tolerable baseline instead of treating one person's standard as automatically superior. Include the less visible tasks: buying detergent, arranging repairs, planning meals and noticing when supplies are low.

You can divide by task, available time or rotating turns. What matters is that an owner takes responsibility for recognizing and completing the work, rather than waiting to be repeatedly reminded. This is where the mental load in relationships discussion becomes relevant, although this guide is specifically about agreements you make before sharing a home.

A useful trial is to plan an ordinary week together: shopping, cooking, laundry and cleaning, not just restaurant dates. Ask each other what felt fair and what nobody thought to do. A practical trial reveals details that imagined equality often misses.

Personal Space, Privacy and Visitors in a Shared Home

Sharing an address does not mean you have to share every minute, password or conversation. Some people unwind through company; others need a quiet room and uninterrupted time. Define what “alone” means without turning it into rejection. You might agree on a closed-door rule, headphones during work or a weekly evening for separate plans.

Guests deserve equal clarity. Is a friend welcome with one hour's notice? What about relatives staying for several nights or someone holding a spare key? Consider overnight visitors, parties, visitors when the other person is sick and whether both people can decline guests in their own home.

A home can belong to both partners without every space, hour or personal boundary becoming communal property.

These conversations are particularly helpful for a moving in with boyfriend checklist or a first shared apartment, because previously invisible expectations will become daily routines.

Sleep, Work Schedules, Pets and First-Time Living Together

Ordinary routines may determine whether a home feels restful. Compare wake-up times, sleep temperature, noise, remote-work calls, commute lengths and access to bathrooms. A night owl and early riser do not have to become identical; they need a workable plan that lets both sleep and get to work.

If pets are involved, discuss the full responsibility—not just the cuddly parts. Who arranges veterinary appointments, pays for emergencies, handles walks, cleans up and finds care during travel? Also check building rules and accessibility needs before committing to a property.

For people seeking first time living together advice, one useful exercise is to spend several consecutive ordinary days together, including a workday and a low-energy evening. Notice practical constraints without treating every irritation as a warning sign. The goal is to learn enough about real life to plan it.

Conflict and Communication: What Happens When Home Doesn't Feel Easy?

Arguments change when neither person can leave for their own apartment afterward. Decide how you will call a pause, where each person can cool down and when you will return to unfinished issues. A pause that includes a return time protects the conversation; unexplained silent treatment often leaves the other person guessing.

For decisions that affect both people, such as a major furniture purchase or overnight guest, agree whether you need two yeses. Make space for a partner to raise concerns about chores and spending before the problem grows. Schedule a check-in after the first month, when your assumptions have been tested by real life.

If disagreements include threats, intimidation, restricting someone's access to the home or preventing them from leaving safely, treat this as a safety concern rather than a communication style. A checklist cannot make coercion safe. If appropriate, consult trusted support and consider independent housing options.

What If We Break Up? Make an Exit Plan Before Signing

Talking about separation does not predict a breakup. It acknowledges that a shared lease can create responsibilities even if a relationship ends. Discuss who can leave, what notice may be required, how deposits are returned, who owns shared belongings and how utilities or subscriptions would be closed or transferred.

An exit plan should be realistic, not a promise that every circumstance will remain friendly. Do not rely solely on “we would figure it out.” Write down the process you currently agree on and check it against local lease terms. Legal rights vary by jurisdiction, by whether one or both partners signed and by the type of housing agreement. Seek qualified local guidance for specific legal questions.

A nonthreatening way to raise the topic

“I want us to build a home together. Part of doing that responsibly is knowing neither of us would be trapped if life changed. Can we talk about what happens to the lease, deposit and furniture if one of us needs to leave?”

After the Checklist: Turn Answers Into Actual Agreements

A good checklist should end with decisions, not thirty polite nods. Make one shared document with three headings: Agreed, Needs Research, and Not Yet Decided. Record what each partner will do, what it costs and the date for a second conversation. Avoid posting passwords or unnecessary personal financial details in shared notes.

If one person is moving into the other's existing home, discuss how to make the new person feel equally at home: storage, décor, keys, guests and reasonable influence over shared spaces. If you are finding a new place together, measure furniture, compare commutes and prepare a moving-week essentials box. Check lease terms and document the condition of the space as local rules allow.

Moving in Together Checklist: Frequently Asked Questions

What should couples talk about before moving in together?

Discuss rent and bills, lease obligations, chores and mental load, guests, privacy, sleep and work routines, pets, conflict habits, and what happens if one person needs to move out. Both people should know which agreements are still unsettled.

How many questions should you ask before moving in together?

There is no magic number. This guide offers 30 practical questions because it covers the main everyday and contractual decisions without requiring a personality test. The quality of your answers matters more than the count.

Should we split rent 50/50 when we move in?

Equal, income-proportional and other negotiated splits can all work. Compare affordability, agreed shared costs and nonfinancial contributions, then choose a method both people understand and can sustain.

Is it too soon to move in together after six months?

There is no universally correct relationship duration. Assess whether you know each other's real routines, can discuss money and boundaries, and understand the housing obligations. If the decision feels pressured, more time may help.

What should we do before signing a lease together?

Read the document, check whose names appear, clarify potential liability, deposits and notice terms, and discuss an exit plan. Housing rules differ by place, so seek local professional guidance if needed.
