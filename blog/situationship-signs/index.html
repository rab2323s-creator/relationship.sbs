Is He Serious About You or Just Wasting Your Time? 7 Signs

If he wants a serious relationship, his actions will gradually match his words: he makes time, follows through, and talks honestly about what he wants. He includes you in realistic plans, respects your boundaries, and handles difficult conversations instead of disappearing. Attraction, daily texts, and big promises alone do not prove commitment. If you are unsure, ask a direct question and judge the answer by what actually changes.

By Laura Aram SmithPublished January 29, 2026Updated October 18, 2026~13 min read

You have a good time together. He texts you, says he misses you, and maybe even talks about what you could do next month. But when you try to figure out whether he actually wants a relationship, nothing is clear. You start wondering: Is he serious about me, or am I wasting my time?

You cannot read his mind, and no list can promise to predict what he will do. But you can notice something far more useful: does his interest turn into reliable, mutual action? The seven signs below help you tell the difference between a connection that is growing and one that stays in the same uncertain place.

This is especially relevant if you are in a situationship—a connection with affection or dating but no shared understanding of what comes next. Being casual by mutual agreement is not wrong. The problem is when one person wants a serious relationship and the other keeps the future unclear.

How to Know If He Wants a Relationship: Start With His Actions

The best way to tell if he wants something serious is to compare three things: what he says he wants, what he consistently does, and whether the connection is moving in a direction you both understand. Attraction can be genuine even when commitment is absent. A busy week or an awkward conversation also does not automatically mean he is wasting your time.

Look at the pattern across ordinary moments—not one exciting date or one disappointing text. If you have never asked about intentions, a calm conversation can clear up more uncertainty than another month of guessing.

The short rule: Serious interest is not constant attention. It is clarity plus follow-through over time. You can value those things without demanding a promise before two people know each other.

7 Signs He Is Serious About You and Wants a Relationship

1. He is honest about what he wants

When you ask what he is looking for, he does not treat the question as an accusation. He might say, “I am looking for something committed, but I want us to keep getting to know each other,” or “I like you and want to see where this goes, but I am not ready for exclusivity yet.” The second answer may not be what you hope for, but it is still clearer than changing the subject.

Real-life example: You ask whether he wants a serious relationship. Instead of telling you that labels are meaningless while expecting exclusive attention, he explains his present intentions and asks about yours.

What to notice: Can you both discuss intentions without being pressured, mocked, or made to feel unreasonable?

2. He makes time for you, even when life is busy

Being serious does not mean answering instantly. People work shifts, care for family, and have unpredictable weeks. But when he wants the connection to grow, he usually helps create opportunities to see you or talk properly rather than leaving every plan to chance.

Real-life example: He has to cancel Friday because of work. He tells you before the last minute and asks whether Sunday afternoon works. That is different from “Sorry, busy” repeated for weeks without another suggestion.

What to notice: Does he make realistic alternatives when something gets in the way? For the specific texting question, see he texts every day but never makes plans.

3. He follows through on ordinary promises

A person does not prove commitment through one big speech. Reliability shows up in smaller moments: returning a call he agreed to make, showing up when planned, respecting an important request, or explaining a change rather than leaving you waiting. Nobody is perfectly consistent, so look for the general pattern and how he handles mistakes.

Real-life example: He says, “I'll call you after dinner,” and follows through—or tells you early that he cannot. If he repeatedly promises things that never happen, the promise itself stops being useful evidence.

What to notice: Are his actions usually easier to trust than his most romantic words?

4. He wants to know who you really are

Someone interested only in the excitement of dating may keep conversations focused on flirting and physical chemistry. A person considering a real relationship also becomes curious about the life he would be sharing: your priorities, friendships, work, worries, values, and boundaries. He listens without needing every answer to match his own.

Real-life example: You mention a stressful presentation or family event. Later, he remembers and asks how it went. He also shares parts of his own life, so emotional effort does not flow in only one direction.

What to notice: Is there mutual curiosity, or does the connection stay at the level of compliments and chemistry?

5. He includes you in realistic future plans

Future plans do not have to mean engagement or moving in together. Early on, they may be as simple as discussing a concert next month or talking about when you can next meet. As the connection develops, a person who wants a relationship can discuss bigger questions without pretending they are already settled.

Real-life example: He asks whether you would like to attend an event with him next month, checks the details, and follows through. Saying “One day we'll travel the world” is pleasant, but it is not the same as taking a step together.

What to notice: Is future talk proportionate to your stage of dating and supported by present effort? If you are attached mostly to who someone could become, compare the guide to falling in love with potential.

6. He respects your boundaries and your pace

A serious partner does not expect you to ignore your needs to keep his interest. You should be able to decline a date, ask for safer sex, say you are not ready for exclusivity, or explain that you need consistency. Respectful behavior matters whether the connection eventually becomes committed or not.

Real-life example: You say you are not comfortable with last-minute invitations. Instead of guilt-tripping you, he asks which days generally work. If he cannot meet your preference, he explains that honestly.

What to notice: Can you say no without being punished? See healthy boundary scripts for practical wording.

7. He can have an uncomfortable conversation and stay engaged

Every growing relationship meets a mismatch: a missed message, different expectations, a misunderstanding, or an awkward question about exclusivity. A good sign is not that conflict never happens. It is that both people can address it, take responsibility where appropriate, and try a reasonable repair.

Real-life example: You say, “I felt confused when you stopped replying after we made plans.” He does not have to agree with your interpretation, but he listens, explains what happened, and talks about how to avoid repeating it.

What to notice: When you communicate a need, does the conversation create more understanding—or only another cycle of avoidance?

5 Things That Do Not Prove He Wants a Serious Relationship

  • He texts you every day. Daily contact can be genuine, but it may also be companionship without shared intentions. Read why constant texting does not always become dating.
  • He gets jealous. Jealousy is not a reliable sign of commitment and should never excuse controlling behavior.
  • He says “I miss you.” Feelings can be real without a decision to build a relationship.
  • You have strong chemistry. Attraction does not resolve differences in availability, goals, or expectations.
  • He talks about a distant future. Without reasonable steps now, a large promise is only a possibility. The future-faking guide explains a different pattern of repeated unrealistic promises.

If he tells you directly that he does not want a relationship, take that statement seriously rather than searching his texts for a hidden yes. Liking someone is not the same as being willing or able to commit.

Can a Situationship Turn Into a Real Relationship?

Yes, but not merely because it has lasted a long time. A situationship is a connection in which romantic or emotional involvement exists without a clear, shared agreement about the relationship. It can change when both people want something different and their actions begin to match that agreement.

The real question is not “How do I make him commit?” It is “Are we both choosing the same kind of relationship?” If only one person wants that future, more waiting cannot create a shared decision.

Is He Losing Interest or Just Busy?

This is one of the questions people ask when they are unsure where a connection is going. A busy person may need longer to reply, but they can still show care through reasonable explanations and alternatives. A person who does not want to progress may keep every possible next step vague. You need a pattern, not a stopwatch.

If communication has noticeably changed, the dedicated article Is He Losing Interest or Just Busy? explores that specific issue in more depth. Here, the more useful question is whether you both agree on what you are building.

What to Say When You Want to Know If He Is Serious

Instead of testing him with silence, jealousy, or a complicated message, try one calm question. Choose wording that fits the stage of your connection:

If you have been dating a little while:

“I like spending time with you. I'm dating because I'd like a relationship that can grow. Is that something you want too?”

If you are already emotionally close:

“I care about what we're building, but I'm not sure we're on the same page. Are you interested in working toward a committed relationship, or would you prefer to keep this casual?”

If you keep receiving vague answers:

“I respect that you may not be ready. I'm looking for something clearer, though, so I need to decide whether this works for me.”

These messages communicate your needs without demanding that the other person say yes. For more examples of how to communicate needs in a relationship without sounding accusatory, see How to Communicate Your Needs.

How to read his answer without guessing

“Yes, I want that too.” Good starting point. Ask what you both mean, then watch whether plans, consistency, and expectations match the conversation.

“I like you, but I'm not ready.” Believe the stated limit. You can continue casually if that genuinely suits you, or choose to step away.

“Why do we have to label everything?” He may simply dislike labels, but you still deserve a conversation about the actual arrangement: exclusivity, effort, contact, and expectations.

He avoids answering at all. You do not need to keep asking the same question indefinitely. Repeated avoidance is information about whether this connection can meet your needs right now.

Two Realistic Examples: Same Attraction, Different Direction

Example A: Maya and Daniel. They have dated for six weeks. Daniel is sometimes slow to text because he works late, but he helps arrange dates and remembers things Maya tells him. When she says she eventually wants a relationship, he says he does too, though he wants to keep getting to know her. They agree to talk again after spending more time together. Nothing is guaranteed, but the direction is understandable.

Example B: Lena and Chris. They have been seeing each other for months. Chris sends affectionate messages and talks about trips they might take, but repeatedly avoids making plans or discussing what he wants. Lena asks directly and hears only, “Let's see.” When the same pattern continues, she decides to stop waiting. She does not need to prove Chris meant to waste her time; she knows the present arrangement does not work for her.

These are illustrative fictional scenarios, not personal accounts or research case studies.

What Relationship Research Can Actually Tell You

In two longitudinal studies, researchers examining commitment, trust, and pro-relationship behavior found support for the idea that everyday actions can provide information about a partner's commitment. The research did not create a seven-sign test for predicting whether someone will become your boyfriend. It does, however, support a practical emphasis on behavior rather than relying on exciting words alone.

Source: Wieselquist, J., Rusbult, C. E., Foster, C. A., & Agnew, C. R. (1999). Commitment, pro-relationship behavior, and trust in close relationships. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 77(5), 942–966.

This article is guidance for making personal decisions, not a scientific test of someone's intentions. Personality, culture, disability, schedules, and relationship goals can change how commitment is expressed. Direct communication and mutual consent matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

How to know if he wants a relationship?

Look for a pattern of clear intentions, reliable plans, mutual effort, respect for boundaries, and willingness to discuss where the relationship is going. A single message or date cannot confirm commitment.

How do I know if he is serious about me or just wasting my time?

Compare his words with his repeated actions. If he offers affection but avoids clarity, cancels without rescheduling, or only contacts you when convenient, ask directly what he wants and decide whether the answer meets your needs.

What are the signs he wants a serious relationship with you?

He communicates his intentions, makes time, keeps promises, gets to know you beyond flirting, includes you in realistic plans, respects boundaries, and works through disagreements.

Can a man like you but not want a relationship?

Yes. Someone can feel attraction or affection and still be unavailable or unwilling to commit. Take his stated intentions seriously and choose whether that fits what you want.

How long should you date before asking where it is going?

There is no universal number of dates or weeks. You can ask whenever the uncertainty affects your choices, especially before making an exclusivity or intimacy decision that matters to you.

Does texting every day mean he wants a relationship?

Not necessarily. Daily messaging shows that you communicate frequently, but it does not replace mutual intentions, plans, reliability, or commitment.

What if he says he is not ready for a relationship?

Believe the limit he communicates instead of waiting for his feelings to change. Ask yourself whether the connection as it currently exists is something you freely want.

Can a situationship turn into a relationship?

Yes, when both people clearly want that change and start acting on an agreed direction. Repeated ambiguity without honest discussion or progress is not reliable evidence of a future relationship.

Is he losing interest or just busy?

Busy periods are common; look for an explanation, reasonable alternatives, and continued effort. A repeated pattern of avoidance or broken plans is more informative than a slow reply.

What should I text him to ask if he wants a relationship?

Try: 'I like getting to know you, and I'm looking for a relationship that can grow. Is that something you want too?' The aim is clarity, not pressure.

The Bottom Line: Is He Serious About You?

A man who wants a serious relationship does not need to behave perfectly or commit after one date. But over time, you should see honest intentions, a place for you in his life, reliability, curiosity, respect, realistic plans, and an ability to talk through problems. When those things are missing, big promises and constant texts cannot fill the gap.

Ask what you need to know. Listen to the answer. Then compare it with how the relationship actually feels and progresses. You cannot make someone choose commitment, but you can choose not to keep waiting for a future that neither of you has agreed to build.
