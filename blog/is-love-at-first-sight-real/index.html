Is Love at First Sight Real? The Psychology of Instant Attraction

You knew almost nothing about them. Yet, for a few seconds, the world seemed to recognize someone your mind had never met.

Can you really fall in love with a stranger at first sight, or does the mind mistake a powerful spark for love? Explore the psychology of attraction, first impressions, eye contact, and what a second meeting can reveal.

Published October 12, 202615 min readrelationship.sbs Editorial

The first time Nora saw Elias, there was a little bell above a bookstore door and a coat caught on its handle. He turned to help a stranger free the fabric. She laughed. It was barely a moment, far too small for a biography. But she remembered the gesture all the way home, as if something more important than a coat had been released.

That evening, she could recall the angle of his smile and the way he had waited without making the stranger feel foolish. She could not tell you his values, his history, or how he behaved when disappointed. Still, she felt the peculiar confidence of a person who thinks she has recognized someone before knowing them.

Perhaps you have experienced your own version: a look across a room, a voice on a train, a first conversation that seemed to run ahead of time. The feeling is not imaginary. The harder question is what the feeling knows.

Nora and Elias are fictional characters used to illustrate a common experience. The example is not a study participant or a clinical case.

Is love at first sight real? The short answer

The sudden feeling people call love at first sight is real as an experience, but research suggests it more often reflects strong initial attraction than a fully developed form of romantic love. A 2017 study linked reports of love at first sight especially to physical attraction, rather than to the combination of intimacy and commitment associated with longer-term love. A spark can become the beginning of love; it cannot by itself prove compatibility, reciprocity, or a lasting relationship.

What does love at first sight actually mean?

Most people use love at first sight to describe a sudden, unusually strong feeling of romantic interest in someone they have only just seen or met. The feeling might include excitement, a wish to get closer, warmth, a sense of familiarity, or the thought that the encounter somehow matters. That experience can be real as an experience. Its interpretation is the part still open to question.

At first sight you may know their face, their movements, the setting, and the impression they make. You do not yet know how they handle disappointment, whether their words match their actions, or what it is like to disagree with them. When someone says “I knew I loved them the moment I saw them,” the sentence may be an honest description of a lasting memory, even though much of the relationship that gives the memory its meaning happened later.

Is love at first sight real? What the 2017 study found

A frequently cited empirical investigation by Zsok, Haucke, De Wit, and Barelds, published in Personal Relationships in 2017, examined people's reports of love at first sight in online and face-to-face dating contexts. The researchers drew on 396 participants and 500 encounters in their collected observations. Reports of love at first sight were linked especially to physical attraction; the results did not show the same accompanying levels of intimacy, commitment, and passion expected in more fully developed romantic love.

That does not make the initial feeling a fraud. It means the scientific evidence in that study fits powerful initial attraction more readily than a complete, already-developed love relationship. The sample and design also matter: an observational study cannot settle what every person means by “love,” or prove that everyone who experiences a spark will feel the same way months later.

One finding is especially useful for the romantic imagination: a first glance can be the beginning of a lasting relationship, but a later happy ending should not be retroactively treated as proof that every necessary element of love was already present in the first second.

Why can you feel instantly attracted to someone you just met?

The mind makes social judgments quickly. Faces, voices, posture, expressions, familiarity, context, and your own hopes can all influence how a stranger initially feels to you. Those impressions need not be irrational, but they contain far less information than a relationship built through shared experiences.

Appearance opens the door—but it doesn't write the relationship

In speed-dating research, Eastwick and Finkel (2008) compared people's stated preferences with whom they actually felt drawn to during brief interactions. What people said they valued beforehand did not consistently predict the qualities associated with their live romantic interest. Initial attraction is more complex than checking boxes on a list.

Your brain completes an unfinished story

When someone is still unfamiliar, you have room to imagine qualities you haven't observed. You may picture conversations, generosity, compatibility, or a future in which they understand you perfectly. Sometimes that hope motivates people to connect. Sometimes it becomes falling in love with potential rather than with the person you are gradually discovering.

A meaningful setting can amplify a feeling

A bookstore in winter, a wedding, a train platform after a difficult week, or a night when you are ready for change can become part of the emotional charge. Context can make a moment memorable without proving it is destiny.

Can eye contact make two strangers fall in love?

Eye contact can signal attention and social engagement. It may contribute to a feeling of connection, particularly when a glance is welcomed and followed by a warm exchange. But prolonged eye contact is not a reliable test of mutual love. A stranger might look at you because they recognize your face, are curious, are listening, or simply happen to turn at the same moment.

Research on initial attraction shows that what people say they want and whom they respond to in person can diverge. That should make us cautious about claims that one gaze activates a guaranteed romantic mechanism. The considerate response to a charged glance is ordinary: smile if it feels appropriate, notice whether interest is mutual, and leave space for the other person not to engage.

When you search “we locked eyes and I felt a connection,” the useful follow-up is not “Does this mean they're my soulmate?” It is “Did we actually interact, and did both of us choose to learn more?”

Why do I feel connected to someone I just met?

Sometimes a conversation with a new person flows so easily that an hour seems to collapse into ten minutes. You may share humor, values, curiosity, or the feeling of finally being understood. These moments can become the beginnings of meaningful relationships, romantic or otherwise. They do not by themselves establish trust.

One practical distinction is between feeling understood in a moment and being reliably understood over time. The first is an impression. The second becomes visible as someone listens, remembers, communicates honestly, and responds with care under a variety of conditions.

If you have felt a rare connection after one meeting, take it seriously enough to explore—not so literally that you ignore contrary information or assume the other person feels the same.

Love at first sight vs a crush vs limerence

These experiences can overlap, but they ask different psychological questions. The point of distinguishing them is not to take poetry out of attraction; it is to help you notice whether the feeling is growing alongside knowledge.

Instant attraction

A strong pull toward someone based on limited exposure. You may want a second encounter without yet knowing much about them.

A crush or infatuation

Excitement, romantic thoughts, and imagined possibilities. The feeling may be brief or may lead to genuine connection.

Limerence

Can involve intrusive preoccupation, uncertainty, and craving for reciprocation. Not every intense crush is limerence. Explore our guide to limerence and its psychology.

Growing love

Attraction can deepen alongside mutual knowledge, respect, reliability, and chosen commitment. No precise timetable makes it official.

The widely discussed triangular theory of love by Robert Sternberg describes intimacy, passion, and decision or commitment as distinguishable components of love. It is a framework, not a diagnostic instrument. Its value here is simple: an intense feeling at first sight may involve passion while leaving other dimensions largely untested.

Can you fall in love after one conversation or a first date?

Some people describe themselves as falling in love after their first genuine conversation rather than their first visual encounter. A conversation supplies something a glance cannot: fragments of the person's actual thoughts, humor, attitudes, and responsiveness. Yet even an exceptional evening offers only a sample of the person and the circumstances.

Think of the difference between these two statements: “I felt an extraordinary connection that night,” and “I knew exactly who they were.” The first can be perfectly sincere. The second makes a claim a first meeting cannot substantiate.

What makes a first meeting promising?

  • You feel comfortable being yourself instead of performing a role.
  • Interest appears mutual, not inferred from a fleeting glance.
  • Conversation contains curiosity, listening, and room for different opinions.
  • You are interested in meeting the real person again—not merely recreating a perfect scene.
  • The person respects your pace, boundaries, and ability to say no.

These aren't scientific “five signs of true love.” They are practical observations that can guide the next step without treating chemistry as evidence of compatibility.

What to do when you feel love at first sight

  1. Let yourself enjoy the feeling. You don't have to belittle a spark just because it doesn't yet qualify as developed love.
  2. Check reality gently. What do you know, and what are you imagining? Keep both categories separate.
  3. Look for mutual interest. A kind interaction or accepted invitation tells you more than studying glances from a distance.
  4. Choose a low-pressure next step. If the context makes it appropriate, introduce yourself or suggest coffee. Accept any refusal without bargaining.
  5. Give consistency time to appear. Look at kindness, communication, boundaries, and how each person behaves after the novelty fades.
  6. Keep your other relationships and routines alive. An early connection shouldn't become the only source of meaning in your week.

Can love at first sight be mutual?

Two people can certainly experience attraction toward each other during an initial meeting. But one person's intensity is not evidence that the second person's experience matches it. Even if both say the encounter felt special, they may mean different things by “special.”

Rather than decoding body language as proof of hidden emotion, look for ordinary signs of reciprocal choice: both people freely continue the conversation, show interest in learning more, and can respect a no or a slower pace. A person can be friendly, affectionate, or attentive without wanting romance.

Does love at first sight last?

Some couples who stay together remember a vivid first encounter; others grow close gradually after neutral or even awkward beginnings. The fact that a relationship endures does not tell us that its initial spark was a reliable prediction, and the absence of an instant spark does not make future love impossible.

This is a problem of looking backward. Once a relationship is meaningful, people can weave its beginning into a beautiful story. That story may be valuable and truthful as memory without being a universal law of romantic attraction.

A more useful test is not “How intense was the first moment?” but “Does this relationship become more mutual, honest, safe, and satisfying as we get to know each other?”

Frequently asked questions about love at first sight

Can you really fall in love at first sight?

You can experience a powerful romantic feeling immediately. Research suggests that what people call love at first sight is more closely associated with initial attraction than the full intimacy and commitment that can develop over time.

What is love at first sight in psychology?

It is the label people give to a sudden sense of romantic connection upon seeing or meeting someone. It is not a clinical diagnosis or a validated prediction of relationship success.

Why do I feel like I know someone I just met?

A stranger may seem familiar because of their expressions, voice, manner, resemblance to past experiences, or your interpretation of a brief interaction. The sense of familiarity is meaningful to notice but not evidence you know the person's character.

Can you feel chemistry the moment you see someone?

Yes, people can feel immediate attraction. Chemistry is a subjective experience and does not, on its own, establish mutual romantic interest or compatibility.

What does it mean when you lock eyes with someone and feel a spark?

Eye contact can feel emotionally intense, but one glance does not reveal the other person's intentions. Mutual interest becomes clearer through direct, respectful interaction.

Is love at first sight the same as lust or a crush?

Not exactly. Attraction, romantic interest, sexual desire and developing love can overlap while remaining distinct. An early feeling provides limited information about how the bond may grow.

Can you fall in love after one conversation?

You may feel deeply moved by one conversation, and it can initiate love. One exchange still cannot reveal all the qualities important to a sustained relationship.

Do both people feel love at first sight?

They might both experience attraction, but one person's feeling cannot confirm another's. Reciprocity requires communication and freely chosen interest.

Does love at first sight last forever?

No first impression guarantees a relationship's future. Some powerful starts grow into lasting love and some don't; other long relationships start with no immediate spark.

Is there a scientific test for love at first sight?

No accepted test can tell whether a glance means love or predict whether two strangers are soulmates. Studies can examine reports and correlates of attraction, not prove destiny.

Research references and how to interpret the evidence

This article draws distinctions between reported feelings, observed associations, and predictions. The opening story is fictional. Some studies use modest or specific samples, and theories of love are frameworks, not diagnostic scoring systems. The term “love at first sight” is subjective, so the research cannot invalidate a reader's memory of an important encounter or confirm that a stranger is a soulmate.

  1. Zsok, F., Haucke, M., De Wit, C. Y. & Barelds, D. P. H. (2017). “What kind of love is love at first sight? An empirical investigation.” Personal Relationships. Observational dating and online reports of love at first sight, linked strongly to attraction.
  2. Eastwick, P. W. & Finkel, E. J. (2008). “Sex Differences in Mate Preferences Revisited: Do People Know What They Initially Desire in a Romantic Partner?” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 94(2), 245–264. Stated mate preferences versus live speed-dating attraction.
  3. Sternberg, R. J. (1986). “A Triangular Theory of Love.” Psychological Review, 93(2), 119–135. Framework involving intimacy, passion, and decision/commitment.
  4. Joel, S., Eastwick, P. W., Finkel, E. J. et al. (2017). “Is Romantic Desire Predictable? Machine Learning Applied to Initial Romantic Attraction.” Psychological Science. Illustrates the difficulty of predicting whom a person will desire from profiles and traits alone.
