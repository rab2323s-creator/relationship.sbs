Should I Get a Prenup? 12 Questions to Ask Before Marriage

Should you get a prenup? Consider one if you want clear, fair rules about property, debt, a business, inheritance or financial support before marriage. It can be useful even if you're not wealthy, especially when one partner may pause their career to care for family. But a prenup is not right for everyone, and signing one does not guarantee it will be legally enforceable. The safest starting point is honest financial disclosure, an unpressured discussion, and independent local legal advice for both partners. This guide explains what a prenup protects, what it cannot do, its risks and costs, and 12 questions to ask before you sign.

Should You Get a Prenup? The Straight Answer

Consider a prenup if you want clarity about assets, debts, a business, inheritance, future earnings, or financial responsibilities before getting married. It may be especially worth discussing if either person owns a home, has children from another relationship, expects to inherit property, is entering a second marriage, or expects one spouse to leave paid work for caregiving. But not every couple needs one, and a prenup is not automatically fair or enforceable just because it is signed.

Ask whether a thoughtful agreement would protect both people under the law that applies to your marriage. Think about the partner who owns a business and the partner who may lose earning power while caring for children. They may need different protections, and the agreement should account for both. American Bar Association guidance stresses that property, support, disclosure, timing and enforceability vary among jurisdictions.

Practical first step:

Have an honest financial conversation well before the wedding, exchange truthful information about assets and obligations, and consult separate qualified family-law advisers where appropriate. Do not present a completed document as a last-minute test of love. An independent lawyer can explain what the law would already provide if you marry without an agreement.

This guide helps you decide whether to explore a prenuptial agreement, how to raise it, what questions to ask a lawyer, and what it cannot do. It is not a fill-in legal form and cannot guarantee that a court will enforce any specific clause.

What Is a Prenup? Meaning, Purpose and the Default Rules of Marriage

A prenuptial agreement—also called a prenup, premarital agreement or antenuptial agreement—is an agreement made in anticipation of marriage that may change certain financial rights and obligations arising from that marriage. Depending on local law, it may address property classification, debts, income, spousal support and the handling of particular assets if the marriage ends or one spouse dies.

Without a prenup, the rules of the jurisdiction govern many of these questions. That does not mean all property will be divided fifty-fifty, that every premarital asset is automatically protected, or that every spouse must pay alimony. The default rules differ; the facts of the marriage matter. The Cornell Legal Information Institute explains that prenups are pre-marriage contracts whose provisions may be limited by public policy.

Is a prenup only for rich people?

No. A family business, an inherited home, student loans, modest savings, or the prospect of leaving work to raise children may matter more to a couple than the total value of an estate. However, a prenup may cost more than it is worth in a particular situation. Ask first what problem it would solve.

Does a prenup mean you're planning to divorce?

Not necessarily. Some couples see it as an exercise in financial planning, much as discussing insurance or a will does not mean they expect disaster. Others find it unsettling. Both responses deserve attention. A legitimate plan must be voluntary and fair, not a way to silence the partner who is worried.

The Blue Ledger: A Story About What We Bring Into a Marriage

On the morning the florist delivered the wrong flowers, Nora found a blue ledger under her mother's kitchen table. The ledger had been in the family longer than Nora had been alive. Inside it were grocery totals from summers nobody remembered, a calculation for replacing a window, and the price of the hospital taxi her mother had paid on the night her father died. There were stains from coffee cups that had long since broken. Her mother had written numbers in the margins as though she could protect the whole household by putting everything in its place.

Outside, rain fell against the narrow street. Her wedding was seven weeks away. There were seating cards on the counter and an unfinished argument in her chest. The evening before, Leo had asked whether they should speak to lawyers about a prenuptial agreement.

Leo owned a small design firm he had spent a decade building. Nora worked as a nurse, and they had talked about having a child. Her salary was steadier, at least for now, and there were evenings when Leo came home smelling of printer ink and fear because a client had not paid. He wanted to protect the business if things ever went badly. She wanted to protect a future version of herself who might leave hospital shifts for a year and lose the promotions she had spent so long earning.

But when he had said the word prenup, she had heard something else: I will love you, provided you never become a problem. She had gone quiet. He had mistaken the silence for a refusal. They had gone to bed without the small ritual of touching hands that usually repaired their days.

At noon Leo arrived with a paper bag of bread still warm from the bakery. He did not bring a contract. He sat at the kitchen table where the blue ledger lay open and waited while Nora explained the sentence she had heard beneath the one he had spoken.

“I wasn't asking you to sign away your life,” he said. “I was frightened that something I built could disappear.”

“And I was frightened,” she said, “that the part of our life I build won't look like property.”

He watched her trace her mother's handwriting: bus fare, medicine, winter coat. Money had kept the family safe, but the list also remembered labor no bank statement could describe. Leo turned to a blank page at the back and drew two columns. On one side he wrote: What I hope to protect. On the other: What you should never have to give up.

They spent the afternoon making no decisions. They wrote down the business and the mortgage, his debt from the first year of the firm, her pension, the possible cost of childcare and the career each person might set aside for the family. At four, they crossed out a sentence that seemed fair to Leo until Nora imagined trying to live under it. At five, she crossed out a sentence that would have kept her safe by making him carry an impossible burden. They agreed to seek separate advice before signing anything.

When he left, the rain had stopped. The florist's replacement flowers had arrived, already opening along their stems. Nora closed the ledger and put it back in the cupboard. It had not become a wedding present or a legal document. It was only evidence that her mother, like most people, had spent a lifetime trying to make enough room for love and the bills that accompanied it.

That night Leo returned to help write the names on the seating cards. They did not talk about whether they would ever separate. They talked about who would take care of whom if life changed shape. The difference did not make the conversation easy. It made it theirs.

Original fictional vignette. Nora and Leo are invented; this is not a case study or proof that a prenuptial agreement will be right for all couples.

Who Should Consider a Prenup? 10 Real-Life Scenarios

“Who needs a prenup?” is not answered by a bank balance alone. Start with the situation that would be difficult to untangle and ask whether a lawful agreement would improve clarity and fairness.

1. You own a business, practice or shares in a company

A founder may worry about valuations, ownership, appreciation or shares held with other partners. An agreement may address how certain interests are classified and valued, depending on law. A business-owning spouse still needs to consider the other partner's sacrifices, household contributions and potential economic disadvantage.

2. One partner owns a house before marriage

A premarital home can raise questions about down payments, mortgage contributions, renovations, increases in value and using joint money on separate property. A house does not necessarily stay simple once two people build a life inside it.

3. You have student loans, personal loans or other debt

A prenup may allocate responsibility between spouses in some circumstances, but it generally cannot rewrite a creditor's rights under a loan or credit agreement. A lender is not automatically bound just because two people privately agree who should pay. Ask a lawyer what the clause actually changes.

4. One of you expects a substantial inheritance or family gift

Family property, heirlooms and gifts may be treated differently across jurisdictions. A prenup may help clarify intentions, but inheritance law and wills need their own consideration. “My parents want it protected” is a reason to talk, not permission to pressure a fiancé.

5. One partner has children from a previous relationship

A remarriage may bring an existing home, inheritance expectations and obligations to children. Estate planning, beneficiary designations and family law may all intersect. A prenup should not attempt to bind a future court's decision on child custody or child support.

6. You expect one spouse to pause a career for children or family care

Career interruptions can affect retirement savings, salary progression and financial independence. A one-sided agreement that protects the higher earner's growing assets without considering this loss deserves serious scrutiny. Talk about a fair process and possible safeguards rather than treating unpaid care as worth zero.

7. You are entering a second marriage

People marrying again may want to protect pre-existing property, existing dependents or estate arrangements without treating their new partner as temporary. Read dating after divorce for how new relationships coexist with established responsibilities.

8. You have very different financial starting points

The goal should not be to make the less wealthy spouse accept every risk. Ask whether the contract would remain reasonable after years of shared life, illness or caring responsibilities.

9. You earn, invest or own property across borders

Multiple citizenships, foreign real estate, international retirement accounts or a possible move can make the applicable law uncertain. You may need family-law advice in more than one jurisdiction, and your agreement may not travel automatically.

10. You both want greater financial transparency

For some couples, the main value of exploring a prenup is a detailed conversation about assets, debt, income, expectations and the financial system they will build. That conversation can be valuable even if lawyers ultimately advise that no agreement is needed.

What Can a Prenup Protect? Assets, Debts, Savings and Spousal Support

A prenup is not a magic shield around every asset. In jurisdictions that recognize the relevant provisions, it may help determine which property remains separate, what becomes marital property and what financial arrangements apply if the marriage ends. Specific clauses may be ineffective or restricted under applicable law.

What Can't a Prenup Do? Child Support, Cheating Clauses and Unfair Terms

Can a prenup decide child custody or child support?

Generally, no. In U.S. practice, children have independent rights, and courts evaluate child support, custody and parenting arrangements under the law and circumstances at the relevant time. A clause cannot bind a court to disregard a child's welfare. ABA guidance makes that limitation explicit.

Can a prenup punish someone for cheating?

So-called infidelity, lifestyle or behavior clauses raise complicated enforcement and public-policy issues. Some may be ineffective, and treating a prenuptial agreement as a system of punishments can produce conflict. Don't rely on an online example to decide whether such a provision will work in your jurisdiction. For the relationship question—what counts as betrayal—see emotional cheating and boundaries, a separate topic from property law.

Can a prenup force a spouse to have children, do chores or stay married?

A prenup is not a tool to compel sexual intimacy, dictate bodily decisions or control daily behavior. The fact that two people can agree to expectations for marriage does not mean those expectations should be inserted as enforceable legal obligations. Use relationship agreements for conversation and lawful legal instruments for legal rights.

Can you waive all spousal support and leave one person with nothing?

Rules about spousal support waivers and severe unfairness vary, but an extreme result may invite legal challenge. Some jurisdictions permit broad bargaining while others restrict support terms or apply safeguards. The fairest conversation includes what happens after illness, disability, years out of paid work or a long marriage—not just a snapshot from the engagement period.

What Makes a Prenup Fair to Both Partners? The Two-Sided Test

A good negotiation asks a harder question than “Does this protect my property?” It asks what the agreement would mean for both futures, including the person who may have less money today or whose contribution is expected to be unpaid.

01

Free choice

Neither person is threatened with immediate cancellation of the wedding, housing loss or financial punishment for seeking advice.

02

Real information

Each knows the other's material assets, liabilities, income and relevant obligations, with disclosures meeting applicable law.

03

Independent understanding

Each has a genuine chance to get their own qualified counsel, ask questions and propose changes without pressure.

Protect the partner who will do unpaid care

Imagine a future in which one spouse steps back from work to care for a child or an aging parent. The other spouse continues to build income and retirement benefits. A prenup written only around premarital balances may fail to reflect what both partners later contribute. Discuss possible care-related support, retirement planning, review provisions and other lawful approaches with counsel.

Compare the agreement with the law you would otherwise have

A signature means little if someone has no idea which legal rights they would otherwise receive. Ask both lawyers to explain what would likely happen without the agreement under the applicable legal regime. That comparison is more informative than a promise that the prenup is “standard.”

Build review points into the conversation

Children, disability, a major inheritance or a move across borders may change circumstances. Ask whether your jurisdiction permits amendments and what formalities would be required. A future review is a discussion plan, not a guarantee that a court will accept a provision.

Is Asking for a Prenup a Red Flag? What the Request—and the Reaction—Can Reveal

Asking for a prenup is not, by itself, a relationship red flag. But how someone asks, the rights they want to change and their response to your questions can tell you whether the process is respectful. This distinction matters especially when one person has more money, legal knowledge or influence.

12 Prenup Questions to Ask Before Marriage (Before You Call a Lawyer)

Use these as a mutual conversation, not as a one-sided interrogation. If you cannot answer a question, write down what information or legal advice is missing.

1. What would we be trying to protect—and why?

Name specific assets, responsibilities or fears. “My business must survive” and “I don't want to be financially stranded after caregiving” are different needs that may both deserve protection.

2. What does each of us own today?

List property, savings, investments, business interests, equity, retirement accounts, valuable assets and any shared ownership. Avoid treating an approximate bank balance as a complete disclosure.

3. What does each of us owe?

Discuss student loans, taxes, credit cards, guarantees, family loans and business liabilities. Clarify what creditors may still enforce regardless of your private agreement.

4. Will premarital property remain separate, and what happens if we use shared money on it?

The most difficult questions often begin after the wedding: a joint mortgage payment on one person's home, renovations, or a business that grows while both contribute to the household.

5. How will future earnings and savings be treated?

Discuss the difference between property brought to marriage and what each of you earns or acquires during marriage. Different local default rules may apply.

6. What if one of us pauses work to raise children or care for relatives?

Talk openly about pension contributions, career progression, support during transitions and whose unpaid labor makes the other person's work possible.

7. How would we handle a business or professional practice?

Ask about valuation, growth, ownership control, business debt, confidentiality and what happens if a company partner or investor has restrictions. Business law and family law may intersect.

8. What do we want to happen to inheritances and gifts?

Consider family expectations, assets held in trust, estate plans and the other spouse's legitimate needs. Don't assume an inheritance is automatically beyond all claims.

9. What would happen if one of us became ill, disabled or financially dependent?

A plan that works when two people are employed may be unjust after a major life change. Ask whether a proposal accounts for realistic vulnerability.

10. What would the law provide if we did not sign a prenup?

Have a qualified local adviser explain the default marital-property, support and inheritance regime. An agreement has meaning only relative to the rights it modifies.

11. Does each of us have enough time, information and separate advice?

Check applicable legal formalities, translation needs, disclosure duties and wedding-date pressures. A rushed signature is not a substitute for informed agreement.

12. Could both of us explain this contract to a trusted person without feeling ashamed or afraid?

The question is not whether outsiders approve. It is whether you understand the real consequences, can say no and believe the process reflects mutual respect. If not, do more work before signing—or decide not to sign at all.

How to Ask for a Prenup Without Making Your Partner Feel Distrusted

If you search “how to ask for a prenup”, the difficult part is often not the vocabulary. It is the fear that your partner will hear “I expect us to fail.” Start early and explain a shared aim. Then stop talking long enough for the other person's reaction.

If you want a prenup and don't know how to begin

“I want us to enter marriage knowing what we're both agreeing to financially. Could we learn about prenups together, find out what the law already does, and decide whether one makes sense for us? I don't want either of us to feel pressured.”

If you own a home or a business

“Part of my financial life existed before we met, and I'd like us to understand how marriage would affect it. I also want to protect the contributions you'll make to our shared life. Can we talk about both sides with separate advisers?”

If your partner feels hurt

“I can see why this sounds like planning for a breakup. I don't want to dismiss that feeling. I want to understand the choices together, and I'm willing to slow down rather than rush you into a decision.”

If you're the partner being asked to sign

“I'm open to understanding what you want to protect, but I won't sign something I haven't reviewed independently. I need full information, enough time and room to propose terms that protect me too.”

If you decide together not to get one

“We looked at our circumstances and the default rules, and for now we don't think a prenup serves us. Let's still agree on how we'll discuss debts, savings and major financial changes.”

A softer tone cannot repair a one-sided document. Communication and legal fairness belong together. If money discussions often turn into blame, read how to talk about money with your partner without fighting and our healthy boundary scripts for conversations about limits and expectations.

How to Get a Prenup: 8 Steps Before the Wedding

  1. Start the conversation early. Give both partners time before deposits, relatives and a wedding deadline make free choice emotionally harder.
  2. Define the purpose. Write down what each person wants protected, including less visible risks such as caregiving-related career loss.
  3. Identify the law that may apply. Ask counsel about residency, place of marriage, property location, potential international moves and any choice-of-law provisions.
  4. Choose qualified, independent advisers. Have each side understand their own interests. Rules about whether counsel is mandatory or can be waived vary.
  5. Gather complete financial information. Assets, liabilities, income, business interests and material obligations may need documented disclosure depending on jurisdiction.
  6. Negotiate terms and realistic future scenarios. Talk through property growth, children, careers, illness, death and retirement, not just who keeps which bank account today.
  7. Review the draft and complete required formalities. Local law may impose writing, signature, witnesses, notarization, minimum review periods or special explanations. Do not treat a generic online checklist as universal.
  8. Keep safe copies and revisit after major changes. Find out what lawful amendment procedures exist. Do not assume an informal email can change the agreement.

The ABA specifically emphasizes early planning, proficient legal counsel and the different formalities involved. Even if you ultimately don't sign, the discovery process may expose important financial questions worth answering before marriage.

How Long Before a Wedding Should You Get a Prenup?

Begin as early as practical—ideally while you still have real time to consider, negotiate, obtain advice and make a free decision. There is no single worldwide “30-day prenup rule” or magic number of weeks. Minimum review periods and other requirements vary by jurisdiction.

California example: Under California Family Code §1615, current in 2026, particular voluntariness findings include an at-least-seven-calendar-day interval between first presentation of the final agreement and signing for agreements executed on or after January 1, 2020, subject to statutory details, plus specific provisions regarding legal advice, voluntariness and informed waiver. Seven days is not a general recommendation for other places and does not guarantee enforceability even in California.

If the wedding is only days away, it may be safer to postpone the agreement than to rush. In some places a postnuptial agreement might be an option after marriage, but postnups have their own rules and may be treated differently. Ask local counsel before making plans around that possibility.

How Much Does a Prenup Cost? Lawyers, Drafting and Online Templates

There is no universal prenup price. Cost depends on the jurisdiction, lawyer experience, assets, complexity, need for valuation, whether the parties negotiate heavily, and how many professionals are involved. An agreement involving a small business, international property or trusts may require substantially more work than a straightforward asset disclosure.

When comparing proposals, ask each lawyer for a written fee structure and which work it includes: initial consultation, financial schedules, draft, negotiation, review, revisions, legal opinion and signing formalities. The lowest advertised number may exclude significant work, while an expensive process is not inherently better.

Equally, some couples may reasonably conclude that a prenup adds little to their existing position. The informed decision is more important than buying a product called a prenup.

Are Prenups Legally Binding? United States vs. England & Wales vs. Germany

Prenup enforceability depends on the applicable law. A sentence that is accurate in one U.S. state may be wrong in another—and very different from the formalities in Germany or the approach in England and Wales. The following is an orientation, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific advice.

JurisdictionBasic positionWhat to check
United StatesState law determines validity and enforcement. There is no single nationwide prenup rule.Disclosure, voluntariness, counsel, signing formalities, support and child-related limits under your state law.

GermanyA marriage contract (Ehevertrag) must satisfy statutory notarization requirements.Notarial form, permissible terms, fairness review, pension and maintenance arrangements under German law.
Other countries and cross-border couplesApproaches vary widely; one country's form may not work in another.Choice of law, location of property, future residence, recognition and local professional advice.

United States: a signed prenup is not always an enforceable prenup

State laws differ on disclosure, unconscionability, timing and legal representation. ABA commentary discusses the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act and why procedural details matter. For a concrete statutory example, read California Family Code §1615, not as a template for every state but as evidence that local rules can be exacting.

England and Wales: fairness still matters

The UK House of Lords Library briefing (2025) explains that agreements are not automatically binding, but courts consider freely entered agreements made with full understanding unless enforcement would be unfair. The UK government's 2026 consultation also describes the position. Reform proposals should not be mistaken for law already in force.

Germany: an Ehevertrag normally requires a notary

German Civil Code §1410 BGB requires a marriage contract to be concluded with both parties present and recorded by a notary. The German Civil Code covers the broader legal framework. A downloaded, privately signed English-language sample is not a substitute for the appropriate German form and advice.

For international couples, ask specialists whether a valid agreement in one place would be recognized where you may divorce, own property or retire. Don't assume that choosing a law in one paragraph resolves all cross-border issues.

The Most Overlooked Prenup Question: What If Love Changes Your Earning Power?

Imagine two people signing a prenup at 27. One owns a promising company; the other earns slightly more from an established job. At 33, they have two children. They agree one parent will work part-time for five years because childcare is expensive and one child needs extra support. By 40, the business has grown, while the other person's career trajectory and retirement savings have changed.

The version of “fair” that seemed obvious at 27 may not describe the actual marriage. The problem is not that success should be punished. It is that financial outcomes are produced in a household where time, earning capacity and unpaid labor are often shared in ways that bank statements do not reveal.

When exploring a prenup, specifically ask what would happen after sustained caregiving, a disability, an extended period of unemployment or a partner's move for the other's career. Possible structures depend on applicable law and must be assessed by professionals; this page does not suggest a universal support formula.

A useful conversation starter:

“If one of us earns much more because the other takes care of our family, how would the agreement recognize that contribution?” This question can surface inequities that a discussion limited to current bank accounts might miss.

Does a Prenup Hurt Trust? Financial Transparency vs. Financial Control

Trust is not the absence of difficult questions. It is the ability to ask them without becoming unsafe. A respectful prenup conversation can clarify assets and debts while making each partner's needs visible. A coercive process can do the opposite, especially when one partner controls the money, the lawyer or the deadline.

Be careful not to confuse financial privacy with hiding important obligations. Partners may choose independent spending accounts while still accurately disclosing material facts for a legal agreement. Concealing debt or ownership in a disclosure process raises concerns both about trust and potential enforceability. For that separate relationship topic, read financial infidelity: secret spending, hidden debt and trust.

If the conversation turns into “If you really loved me, you'd sign,” return to agency: a person can love someone and still insist on their own lawyer. Similarly, a person can love someone and decide the proposed contract isn't acceptable. Read why I don't trust my partner for exploring the evidence behind a broader trust concern, rather than treating a contract as proof of devotion.

A helpful step is a calm money conversation that starts with facts, values and future responsibilities. Then take legal questions to the appropriate adviser.

Should I Get a Prenup? Frequently Asked Questions

Should I get a prenup before marriage?

Consider one if you want legal clarity about a business, house, debts, inheritance, future income or a caregiving-related sacrifice. Compare local default rules with a fair proposal, obtain appropriate independent advice and decide together without pressure.

Is a prenup worth it if I'm not rich?

It can be, depending on what you own, owe or expect to build. A small business, student loans, a home or career interruptions can create important questions. Sometimes the cost and complexity may outweigh the benefit.

Is asking for a prenup a red flag?

Not by itself. Pressure to sign immediately, hidden financial information, threats or blocking independent legal advice are more concerning than simply asking to explore an agreement.

What does a prenup protect?

Depending on the law, it may address premarital or marital property, a business, certain debts, income, inheritance or spousal support. It is not a guarantee that every clause will be enforced.

Can a prenup protect future earnings?

Some jurisdictions permit agreements addressing earnings and assets acquired during marriage. Limits and safeguards vary, especially where future caregiving or financial hardship is involved.

Can a prenup protect me from my spouse's debt?

A prenup may allocate responsibility between spouses in certain situations, but it generally cannot change a creditor's rights or override mandatory law. Ask local counsel about the specific debt and contract.

Can a prenup decide child custody or support?

Generally not in U.S. family-law practice. Courts and law protect children's interests, which cannot simply be waived by two adults in advance.

Can a prenup prevent alimony?

Some jurisdictions permit provisions concerning spousal support, but waivers or limits may face legal restrictions and fairness review. The result depends on local law and circumstances.

How long before the wedding should you sign a prenup?

Start early enough for full information, independent advice and unpressured negotiation. Some places have specific requirements; California, for example, has a statutory seven-calendar-day provision for certain agreements, but that is not a general worldwide rule.

How much does a prenup cost?

There is no universal price. Cost depends on your jurisdiction, number of lawyers, assets, legal complexity and negotiations. Request itemized quotations rather than relying on online averages.

Do both people need a lawyer for a prenup?

Rules differ, but separate qualified legal advice is a strong safeguard. In some jurisdictions legal representation or a particular waiver process may be relevant to enforceability.

Can I write my own prenup or use an online template?

A template may help frame questions, but it may miss required disclosures, timing, signing formalities or local law. Have a qualified professional review any agreement before relying on it.

Are prenups legally binding in the United States?

Potentially, subject to state law. Voluntary signing and relevant disclosure, advice and formality requirements affect enforcement. There is no single nationwide rule.

Are prenups legally binding in the UK?

In England and Wales, they are not automatically binding, though courts may give them substantial weight when freely made with full understanding, unless unfair. Scotland has different law.

Are prenups valid in Germany?

German marriage contracts (Eheverträge) are recognized within legal limits, but BGB §1410 requires formal notarization with both parties present. Local legal and notarial advice matters.

What if I refuse to sign a prenup?

You have a right to seek advice, suggest alternatives and decline a proposal. Your partner may also have a legitimate preference about the financial terms of marriage. Discuss the mismatch rather than signing under pressure.

Can you get a prenup after you're married?

A prenuptial agreement is made before marriage. In some places a postnuptial agreement may be available afterward, subject to separate legal rules and scrutiny.

Does a prenup mean a couple will divorce?

Not necessarily. A prenup is a financial planning instrument and does not predict a couple's future. The quality of the conversation, mutual consent and the terms matter more than the existence of a document alone.
