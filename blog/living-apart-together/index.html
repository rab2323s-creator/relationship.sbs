Living Apart Together (LAT): What It Means, Pros, Cons & 12 Rules

What is living apart together (LAT)? It is a relationship in which two people are committed partners but maintain separate homes rather than one shared household. Living apart doesn't automatically mean less love, a trial separation or a long-distance relationship. Some couples choose two homes for independence, children or later-life priorities; others are apart because of work or cost. Whether LAT works depends on mutual choice, reliable care, money, visits and honest plans. This guide explains the research, pros and cons, 12 practical agreements, married couples, and how to decide if it fits your relationship.

Living Apart Together Meaning: What Is a LAT Relationship?

Living apart together (LAT) describes a couple who consider themselves romantic partners but maintain separate homes instead of one shared household. Some couples choose this arrangement permanently because they value autonomy; others live separately temporarily because of work, family, finances or housing. A LAT relationship can be serious, exclusive, long-term or married. Its defining feature is an ongoing partnership across two households, not a lack of commitment.

Imagine two people who have dinner three nights a week, show up during illness, know each other's families and plan holidays together, but sleep at their respective addresses most nights. That may be LAT, even if they live only fifteen minutes apart. Distance between houses is not the definition; the combination of a relationship and separate homes is.

Is living apart together the same as being single?

No. LAT partners identify as a couple and may share a substantial emotional and practical life. Because many surveys use households to count family arrangements, some people in LAT relationships are classified as living alone in housing data. That is why a household statistic alone cannot tell you whether a person has a partner.

Is LAT a new dating trend?

The term may be new to you, but the arrangement has been studied for decades. Bowling Green State University's National Center for Family & Marriage Research notes that definitions vary: many researchers focus on unmarried partners in separate homes, while others include married couples who are not separated. A useful first question is not “Do we count?” but “Have we clearly agreed on what our relationship means?”

The Evening With Two Keys: An Original Story About Staying in Love Without Sharing an Address

On the third Monday of October, the rain arrived before the baker. It made the city look as though someone had washed its windows and forgotten to dry them. In the fourth-floor apartment above the stationery shop, Clara stood in her slippers waiting for the kettle to stop. A pair of unfamiliar shoes sat beside the door. They belonged to someone she loved, and that was precisely why she was trying not to move them.

At fifty-eight, she had learned how much of a life could be held in a very small space. On the kitchen wall was a pencil mark made by her daughter when she was ten. A cracked blue bowl held letters from her mother, never sorted, never discarded. After twenty-six years of marriage and four quiet years living alone, Clara knew the sound the building made at night. She had discovered that solitude could be a room with open windows rather than an empty chair.

Daniel had come into her life on a Saturday that began with a broken bicycle chain. They were now two years into a relationship that included birthdays, unremarkable Tuesdays, a winter fever, the patient introduction of adult children, and an argument about train tickets that had lasted longer than it deserved. He lived across the river, fifteen minutes away when the buses ran on time. He kept his late father's tools in his hallway and took care of a sister whose health changed with the weather.

Everyone asked when they were moving in together. Her daughter asked gently. His friends asked as if the answer had been delayed in the post. A colleague said that two apartments sounded like an expensive way of avoiding commitment.

For a while Clara began to hear the questions even when nobody spoke. She imagined packing the blue bowl. She imagined deciding which home would be called theirs and which person's life would be expected to fit in the spaces left over. The love between them was steady, but the thought of moving made her strangely small.

One rainy evening Daniel put two keys on the table. One was hers, which she had given him so he could water the plants. The other was his, with a green ring that had belonged to his father. “Do you ever worry,” he asked, “that this makes it look as if we haven't chosen each other?”

Clara looked at the keys. They were ordinary, scratched, warm from his pocket. “I worry,” she said, “that we're being asked to prove something to people who don't live our lives.”

They did not make an announcement that night. They made a calendar. Tuesdays at her place, Fridays at his. One Sunday each month left open for visiting family. They wrote down who would come if one of them was sick. They agreed to ask again in six months whether the arrangement still felt mutual. Daniel said he wanted her to be at the hospital if he needed her, and that frightened her more than a shared lease because it was a promise that mattered.

At eleven he went home. Clara watched the green light of the bus travel along the wet street until it disappeared beyond the bridge. She felt the slight sting of missing him, but it was not the same thing as being abandoned. He sent a message after reaching his apartment: “The kettle survived without you.” She laughed so loudly that the neighbor upstairs knocked once on the floor.

The next morning she put the two keys back in the bowl. Nothing had been solved forever. They still had two heating bills, two sets of laundry, and the difficulty of deciding where Christmas would begin. But when Clara reached for the telephone she was not calling to ask whether he loved her. She was calling to ask whether he had remembered to buy milk.

Sometimes a shared life does not begin with a common front door. Sometimes it begins when two people become trustworthy at arriving, and trustworthy at leaving.

Original fictional literary vignette. Clara and Daniel are invented characters; the story illustrates practical choices, not research findings or a prescribed relationship model.

Living Apart Together vs. Long-Distance Relationships vs. Sleep Divorce

Several search terms sound similar but answer different questions. This is where people often mistake LAT for an unhappy marriage or a relationship that has not progressed. They are not automatically interchangeable.

Why Do Couples Choose to Live Apart but Stay Together?

There is no single LAT personality or motive. The important difference is between living separately by mutual choice and living separately because circumstances leave little option. Some couples combine both.

1. Independence without giving up intimacy

For some partners, a private home supports creativity, work, rest or recovery from a long period of caregiving. The preference may be entirely compatible with affection: “I want a home that is mine” does not necessarily mean “I don't want you in my life.” The real question is whether both people feel actively chosen.

2. A second relationship after divorce or widowhood

Someone who has already raised children, settled into a neighborhood and rebuilt routines may not want another shared household. They may nevertheless want loyalty, trips, family involvement, affection and a future. Dating after divorce can involve negotiating how much independence each partner wishes to preserve.

3. Children and established family homes

Parents with children from earlier relationships may want to avoid abruptly combining schools, routines, bedrooms and relatives. For them, maintaining two homes can be one way of keeping stability, although it also creates travel and scheduling obligations. Our dating someone with kids guide covers boundaries in blended-family relationships.

4. Work, housing and financial constraints

Two homes may reflect careers in different cities, care obligations, an existing mortgage, unaffordable rents or residence rules. This can look like a long-distance relationship, especially when one or both wish to move eventually. Calling an arrangement “a lifestyle choice” should not erase material constraints.

5. Different rhythms of daily life

Some partners have markedly different schedules, sensory needs or levels of desired quiet. Separate residences can reduce friction, but they will not automatically resolve disrespect, unresolved conflict or unmet emotional needs. You still have to talk about the relationship you are building.

A 2024 study using Spanish fertility survey data found that reasons for LAT varied across life stages: younger people often saw it as transitional, while autonomy and previous family experiences were more salient in midlife. These are population patterns, not rules about any particular couple.

Married but Living Separately: Is It Still a Real Marriage?

Yes, partners can remain married while living in separate houses. Their legal marriage status is not dissolved simply because they have different addresses. Whether they count as “living apart together,” legally separated, or in another category depends on their circumstances and on the definition used in the relevant legal or research setting. Two married adults can be living separately because of work, caregiving, mutual preference or relationship breakdown; those are not the same situation.

In a 2025 Family Profile from Bowling Green State University, researchers analyzed U.S. newlyweds not living with their spouse for reasons other than separation. They reported that 12.5% fit that definition in 2023. Crucially, the study did not say 12.5% of all married couples intentionally keep separate homes for independence. Newlyweds, geographic separation and the reasons behind it need to be distinguished.

Can a marriage work without living together?

It can be meaningful and stable when both partners are willing and their commitments remain specific. But marriage is not only a feeling. If you maintain separate households, discuss who responds during illness, how you handle important financial decisions, where documents are kept, what family members understand, and when the arrangement will be reviewed.

Can spouses have different addresses for taxes, benefits or insurance?

The treatment of addresses, dependents, benefits, inheritance and medical decisions varies greatly across countries, states and programs. Do not assume separate addresses eliminate spousal obligations or automatically qualify someone for a particular benefit. Ask a licensed local professional or the relevant agency before making legal or tax decisions. A relationship article cannot determine your eligibility.

Does Living Apart Together Work? What Research Finds—and What It Cannot Prove

Research on LAT should make readers more curious, not give them a universal happiness guarantee. Different studies use different definitions, ages, countries and methods. A positive finding among older adults cannot be applied unchanged to a newly dating 25-year-old couple.

2011–23

Years of UK longitudinal data analyzed by Hu and Coulter in a study of adults aged 60–85. The study compared LAT with marriage, cohabitation and singlehood rather than assuming one living arrangement is best.

The 2025 UK study on LAT and older adults' mental health used fixed-effects models with 93,885 observations from 15,237 people aged 60–85. Older adults in LAT relationships generally had better mental-health outcomes than when single, but the study found little difference in mental health between LAT, cohabiting and married partnerships. Transitioning into or out of relationships also mattered.

A separate U.S. study of adults aged 51 and older, using the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study, likewise reported no significant difference in depressive symptoms between LAT respondents and cohabiting or married adults. This supports taking LAT seriously as a relationship type; it does not mean two homes cause mental-health benefits.

For an important contrast, Nishikido and Castro-Martín (2024) found that young adults in Spain often treated LAT as a temporary stage before co-residence, while midlife couples more frequently had autonomy-related or previous-family reasons. Life stage changes the story.

Finally, the German Centre of Gerontology's corrected report examines LAT in the second half of life and whether people wish or expect to move in with a partner. The corrected version matters because the earlier publication was withdrawn due to a dataset error. Always check the version before repeating an estimate.

The useful research conclusion: separate households do not automatically signal low commitment or poor wellbeing. Equally, living apart is not a proven fix for a relationship that already lacks safety, mutual investment or communication.

Living Apart Together Pros: 8 Possible Benefits of Two Homes

Benefits depend on resources, health, choice and the quality of the relationship. Consider them possibilities, not promises.

  1. More control over daily routines. Each person can choose sleep hours, quiet time, social gatherings and the rhythm of their household without negotiating every detail.
  2. Less household friction. Conflicts over dishes, noise and storage may decline when those tasks are no longer shared daily. This does not remove the need for fair agreements about visits and unpaid care.
  3. Space for established identities. After divorce, widowhood or years of parenting, a home can represent continuity and hard-won independence.
  4. Intentional time together. Dates, visits and overnights may become chosen rather than automatic. The danger is that the relationship feels like perpetual weekends rather than a partnership through ordinary difficulty.
  5. Stability for children and family routines. Separate households may avoid immediate blended-family disruption, though scheduling must remain thoughtful.
  6. Different work and sensory needs can coexist. A night-shift worker and an early riser may feel less physically exhausted if they are not forcing incompatible routines into one space.
  7. Privacy without secrecy. Each partner retains private space while still being honest about commitments, expectations and household involvement.
  8. Flexibility in later life. Some older partners prefer to retain long-established homes and family obligations while forming a meaningful new partnership.

These are potential advantages, not claims that people living apart are happier than those who live together. Compare any benefit to the actual cost and to the partner's experience of the arrangement.

Living Apart Together Cons: 9 Challenges Couples Often Underestimate

  1. Two sets of household bills. Separate rent, mortgages, utilities, insurance, internet and supplies can be a major financial burden.
  2. Travel time and unequal effort. One partner may repeatedly cross town, arrange childcare, pay fares or carry a bag between addresses while the other stays comfortable.
  3. Missing ordinary companionship. When someone has a bad Tuesday, a quick conversation on the sofa may be harder to replace with a scheduled visit.
  4. Uncertainty about the future. One person may see LAT as permanent while the other quietly expects to move in within a year.
  5. Uneven domestic and caregiving labor. Having two addresses does not prevent one partner from becoming the planner, driver or default helper.
  6. Different ideas about privacy and exclusivity. Two homes do not make expectations about dating others, guests or overnight stays self-evident.
  7. Family misunderstanding or stigma. Relatives may read separate homes as proof of low commitment, or ask when the “real” relationship will begin.
  8. Emergencies and access. If one partner becomes ill, somebody needs to know who to call, how to get inside and which choices the partner is legally allowed to make.
  9. Distance can conceal a pattern rather than solve it. Avoiding necessary conversations, inconsistent attention or repeated broken agreements will remain relationship issues in any number of homes.

If your strongest reason for wanting two homes is that you feel consistently unheard in the same room, also read why you feel lonely in your relationship. The cause of emotional loneliness is not always the floor plan.

12 Living Apart Together Rules That Make Two Homes Feel Like One Relationship

There is no standard LAT contract. These twelve topics help partners develop a genuinely mutual agreement instead of relying on hopeful assumptions.

1. Define the relationship, not just the addresses

Are you exclusive, non-exclusive, married, engaged or committed without plans to marry? What would each person say if a friend asked where the relationship is going? A clear commitment is possible without one household, but it requires words.

2. Say whether living apart is temporary or a chosen long-term structure

A “maybe one day” can mean five different things. Describe your present preference, any future hopes, and what would make you reconsider. Neither partner should be kept waiting on a promise the other does not intend to revisit.

3. Agree on visits and overnights without turning affection into attendance

Decide whether you want a predictable rhythm, spontaneous plans or both. For some, three evenings a week feels generous; others want most nights together. There is no correct minimum. Account for work, travel, energy, children and private time.

4. Share travel costs and effort fairly

If one person takes the train every time, fairness may require changing the schedule, contributing to costs or hosting more often. Equal number of journeys is not always equitable when work or caring responsibilities differ.

5. Plan how you will handle illness and emergencies

Who is the first call after a fall, a fever or a hospital admission? What will you do if a partner cannot safely stay alone? A healthcare proxy or permission to receive updates may require formal documents according to local law.

6. Set clear boundaries for keys, belongings and unexpected visits

A spare key for emergencies is not open permission to enter at any hour. Discuss what items live where, whether visits need advance notice and how to preserve space without making your partner feel unwelcome.

7. Discuss money without assuming legal separation means financial independence

Do you split costs of visits, holidays, groceries and repairs? Are you pooling savings for a future? Do you have legal or family obligations that affect each other? If married, addresses alone do not automatically change those obligations.

8. Decide what everyday communication is enough

One person may like a good-morning call; another may prefer a long conversation every few evenings. Specify how you signal a difficult day and what a reasonable response looks like, without demanding constant availability.

9. Make room for intimacy and physical affection

How do you maintain sexual and nonsexual closeness around privacy, health and energy? Do not turn visits into an obligation to be romantic or sexual. Consent and changing needs matter whether there is one bedroom or two addresses.

10. Include children, relatives and social circles respectfully

Decide how you will participate in birthdays, holidays, care tasks and family decisions. Where children are involved, prioritize their routines and avoid implying that a new partner owes them an immediate parental role.

11. Keep conflict repair possible

Going home after a hard conversation may be helpful when both people agree to return to it. It becomes damaging when leaving is a way to punish, evade accountability or end every difficult discussion without resolution. See our stonewalling versus healthy timeout guide.

12. Schedule a review date

Agree to revisit the arrangement every few months or after major changes: new jobs, a child leaving home, illness, a move or altered finances. Ask if both are still choosing the arrangement. Freedom to change your mind is part of an honest agreement.

Can You Be in Love and Live Apart? Intimacy, Trust and Feeling Lonely

Yes: you can love someone and prefer separate homes. Emotional intimacy means being able to know, support and be known by the other person. It does not require identical addresses. But distance can make reliable care harder to observe, especially if the partnership is already uneven.

The simplest useful distinction is between chosen space and unwanted absence. Chosen space makes room for personal life while leaving the connection dependable. Unwanted absence means one person repeatedly experiences uncertainty, avoidance or loneliness while the other insists everything is fine.

How often should LAT couples see each other?

There is no evidence-based number of nights that defines a healthy LAT relationship. A pair living across town might see each other several times a week; people in different cities may schedule longer stays. Evaluate whether the pattern feels mutual, financially sustainable and responsive when life becomes difficult.

Does sleeping separately keep romance alive?

It may make private time and reunions more intentional for some couples. But it is not established that separate homes reliably improve sexual satisfaction, prevent boredom or create long-term attraction. Feeling desired is not identical to feeling cared for. The intimacy in long-term relationships guide explores the different reasons closeness changes over time.

What if I feel lonely although we are together?

Ask whether your partner reliably responds to good news, bad news, family decisions, ordinary requests and conflict—not only planned date nights. Our article Why Do I Feel Lonely in My Relationship? addresses loneliness that can happen inside either a shared home or two separate ones.

Is Living Apart Together Healthy Independence—or Avoidant Attachment?

A preference for living alone is not a diagnosis and cannot, by itself, tell you a person's attachment style. Some people need quiet, independence, sensory predictability or continuity after a previous marriage. Others want two homes because that makes difficult conversations easier to postpone. Those motives may coexist.

Instead of labeling a partner as avoidant, look at behavior: Do they make plans and keep them? Can you talk about needs without punishment? Are they available during genuine difficulty? Can you disagree about the arrangement without fear? Does the separate-home choice belong to both of you?

Someone can be close and reliable while needing more private space. Someone can also live with you and be emotionally absent. If you find yourself wondering whether the issue is intimacy rather than housing, compare our signs of emotional unavailability and why avoidant-leaning partners pull away. Neither page can diagnose your partner from a household arrangement.

Living Apart Together After Divorce, With Children, or After 50

LAT after divorce: protecting two already-full lives

After a divorce, a new partner may arrive when routines, homes, family friendships and finances are established. Separate homes can preserve continuity while a couple decides what to combine and what to keep separate. Avoid treating an unwillingness to cohabit as proof of a rebound or a lack of seriousness. Ask about the actual commitment and plans instead.

LAT and children: a relationship is also a scheduling system

School nights, custody arrangements, transport, privacy and children's feelings all matter. New partners should not assume access to children's homes or caregiving roles. Where children are older, maintaining separate spaces may help avoid a rushed blended household; with younger children, it can make visits and caregiving more complicated. Each family needs a child-centered conversation.

LAT after 50 or 60: autonomy alongside partnership

For some older adults, LAT provides closeness without dismantling a long-established home. The 2025 UK longitudinal study found that LAT was a meaningful partnership form in later life, rather than simply equivalent to singlehood. This is especially relevant for people balancing adult children, aging parents, retirement arrangements and caregiving.

Age doesn't make the decision automatically wise or risky. It changes the questions: who drives at night, who helps after surgery, whose home has stairs, and what practical support can each partner genuinely provide?

What Happens When One LAT Partner Gets Sick? Care, Emergency Access and Planning

Two homes are easiest to romanticize when everybody is well. The practical test often arrives with a fever, a fall, a surgery or a change in mobility. A dependable partner does not need to live at your address, but the couple needs to know what help can and cannot be offered.

  1. Exchange emergency contacts and confirm whether you want to be contacted first.
  2. Discuss access to each home and pets or dependents who would need care.
  3. Keep essential information secure, current and accessible by agreement—not through surveillance.
  4. Identify backup support so one partner isn't the only possible caregiver.
  5. Ask about formal legal authority for medical decisions and information sharing where relevant.
  6. Review whether the living arrangement remains accessible after a health change.

Don't assume that an unmarried partner automatically has legal medical decision-making rights. Conversely, being married and having separate addresses can carry obligations that require jurisdiction-specific advice. Our chronic illness and relationships guide goes deeper into supporting a partner while avoiding control and caregiver burnout.

Does Living Apart Together Affect Taxes, Benefits, Property or Inheritance?

LAT is a social relationship description, not a single legal status recognized identically everywhere. Legal consequences depend on whether partners are married, registered partners or unmarried; where they live; and which program or contract is involved.

Questions worth taking to a qualified local adviser include: If we marry but retain different addresses, which household information must we report? Would moving in or sharing money change benefit eligibility? Who inherits a home? Who may access medical information? What happens to a shared purchase or lease? What protection exists for dependents?

A 2023 scholarly review of legal reforms for cohabitants and LAT partners describes how LAT arrangements do not fit easily into traditional legal categories. That is an argument for caution, not a statement of what any individual must file or claim.

Important legal distinction:

“We don't live together” is not an answer to a tax, eligibility, custody or inheritance question. Don't make benefits or legal declarations based on a generic blog article. Check the official rules that govern your location and status.

How to Tell Your Partner You Want to Live Apart but Stay Together

The most difficult part may be explaining that your desire for a different home arrangement is not a request for less love. Start with what you are choosing, not what you are rejecting. Then leave real room for your partner's response.

When you genuinely want a long-term LAT relationship

“I love being your partner, and I want us to keep building a future. I've noticed that having my own home is important to how I feel grounded. Can we talk about whether staying together in two homes could work for both of us—and what you would need to feel secure?”

When you want to live apart temporarily

“I don't want to pretend the housing situation is easy. I'd like us to work out a practical plan while we have separate homes, and choose a date to review whether moving in is realistic.”

When your partner fears it means rejection

“I understand why this could sound like distance from you. I don't want your feelings to be an afterthought. Can we talk about what commitment would look like day to day—not only where we sleep?”

When you don't want LAT

“I respect your need for independence, but sharing a home is important to me. I need us to be honest about whether there is a future that works for both of us, rather than promising something neither of us can offer.”

When you need more contact

“I don't need us to share an address tomorrow, but I do need predictable time together and support when life is hard. Could we agree on a rhythm and try it for a month?”

A request to live apart should not become an ultimatum disguised as self-care. Equally, wanting to share a home is a legitimate relationship preference. For language that supports a specific request, see how to communicate your needs.

7 Signs a Living Apart Together Relationship May Not Be Working

  1. Only one person chose the arrangement. One partner feels permanently on hold while the other refuses to discuss the future.
  2. The separation is used as punishment. A person disappears after conflict and returns only when the other gives up their concern.
  3. Visits happen on one person's terms. One partner controls all dates, places and communication, leaving the other chronically uncertain.
  4. Care is missing outside romantic occasions. Someone offers wonderful weekends but is absent during illness, grief or ordinary stress.
  5. Costs or effort are consistently unfair. Transportation, childcare, planning or financial sacrifice fall on one partner without acknowledgment or change.
  6. Important expectations are deliberately kept vague. Exclusivity, children, marriage or a future move are never addressed because the ambiguity benefits one person.
  7. Safety concerns are present. Threats, controlling behavior, coercion, intimidation or stalking remain serious regardless of where people sleep.

None of these signs proves a partner is bad or a specific attachment type. They indicate a need for conversation, boundaries and sometimes outside support. In coercive or unsafe situations, safety takes precedence over efforts to negotiate the ideal housing model. For a separate guide to patterns of harm, see toxic relationship warning signs.

Living Apart Together FAQs: Direct Answers People Search

What does living apart together mean?

Living apart together (LAT) means two people identify as partners while maintaining separate households. It can be temporary or long-term, chosen or shaped by circumstances.

Can a relationship work if you don't live together?

Yes, it can. Commitment, communication, mutual choice, practical support and clear plans matter more than sharing a postal address alone.

Is living apart together healthy?

It can be healthy when both partners freely choose the arrangement and feel cared for. It can be painful if one person feels rejected, misled or chronically alone. Living separately is not itself a clinical sign.

Can you be married and live in separate houses?

Yes. A couple can remain married with separate homes. Legal, financial and benefit implications depend on local laws and individual circumstances; separate addresses don't automatically end a marriage.

Is LAT the same as a long-distance relationship?

Not necessarily. LAT emphasizes maintaining separate households while partnered. Long-distance relationships emphasize geographic separation and may include plans to live together in the future.

Is living apart together the same as sleep divorce?

No. Sleep divorce commonly means separate beds or bedrooms within one household. LAT means two homes.

How often do living apart together couples see each other?

There is no required frequency. Partners may meet several times weekly, on weekends or less often, depending on location and life circumstances. What matters is an agreed rhythm that supports both people.

Do LAT couples have better relationships or more sex?

There is no sound basis for promising that two homes improve every relationship or sexual connection. Some studies find comparable mental-health outcomes for older LAT, married and cohabiting adults, but results vary by population and method.

Why would a married couple choose to live apart?

Possible reasons include independence, work, caring responsibilities, health needs, established family homes or a mutually preferred lifestyle. Some married couples are apart involuntarily, so don't assume every case is freely chosen.

Is living apart together common after 50?

Researchers have documented LAT among older adults, often linked to existing households, family responsibilities and independence. The frequency varies by country, sample and definition.

Does LAT mean my partner is avoidantly attached?

No. An address cannot identify attachment style. Look instead at consistent care, respect for boundaries, conflict repair and whether your preferences are mutually acknowledged.

What if one partner wants LAT and the other wants to move in?

Neither preference is automatically wrong. Discuss what each needs and whether a workable compromise exists. A permanent mismatch about shared housing may be a meaningful compatibility issue.

What happens if a LAT partner gets sick?

Partners should agree on emergency contacts, care arrangements, access and backup support. Formal decision-making authority may require legal documentation depending on the location and relationship status.

Does living apart together save money?

Often two households mean duplicated housing and utility costs, although actual costs vary. Compare realistic budgets including transport and care before deciding.

Can you live apart together forever?

Some couples choose LAT as an enduring arrangement. It remains important to revisit it as health, finances, family needs and preferences change.
