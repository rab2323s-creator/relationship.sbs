The Book That Stayed Open

The rain had stopped just before six, but the little bookshop still smelled of wet wool and cold pavement. It was the sort of place where people lowered their voices without being asked, as though the books might otherwise forget what they were holding. Mara was thirty-two, and she had rehearsed three cheerful things to say on her walk there. By the time she reached the second-hand poetry shelf, every one of them had vanished.

Beside the window, a man in a green jacket was looking at a book with the same faded blue cover as one she had been carrying for ten minutes. She could have said the line she had read online about mysterious strangers. Instead she said, “Does yours have the page that falls out?”

He looked down, surprised. “Page forty-seven?”

“That's the one.”

They laughed quietly, not because either of them had been clever, but because they had found an oddly specific piece of common ground. They talked about the book, then about the city, and then about which bus always arrived early until you needed it. Mara noticed that he asked questions too. When she looked toward the door, he did not step into her path.

“I should catch my train,” she said. “But I've liked this conversation.”

He nodded, smiling. “Me too.” After a beat, he asked whether she might want to visit the next bookshop reading together. She considered it, without being hurried. “Yes,” she said, “I'd like that.”

On the train home, Mara could not remember whether she had looked charming, where she had put her hands, or whether her laugh had sounded strange. She remembered the exact moment she had stopped trying to impress him and started wondering what he thought. In her bag, the old book remained open at page forty-seven, its loose leaf tucked carefully inside.

What Does Flirting in Person Actually Mean?

Flirting in person means expressing possible romantic interest through a combination of friendly conversation, attention, warmth, humor and—when it feels appropriate—clear verbal interest. It does not require touching, sexual jokes or a special “technique.” It is only enjoyable when the other person has genuine freedom to take part or not.

In everyday life, flirting may look almost identical to friendliness at first. The difference often becomes clearer through sustained mutual engagement and a respectful invitation. Even then, the most reliable answer is what the other person says and chooses, not a claim that you can read their thoughts.

The easiest face-to-face flirting formula

Notice → Invite → Listen → Share → Check → Ask clearly. Notice something real, invite a small conversation, listen, share your side, check for mutual engagement, then make one simple invitation if the context is right. Stop when the participation is not mutual.

How to Start Flirting in Person Without Being Awkward

If you freeze when you see someone you like, your goal is not to become fearless. Your goal is to make the first step small enough to take. Use a real shared context: a class, a group event, a mutual friend's introduction or a public activity with natural downtime. Ask a question that is easy to answer; then focus on the response rather than your next line.

1

Take one slow breath, relax your shoulders and remember that a conversation is not an audition.

If the conversation is short or awkward, that does not mean you are inherently bad at flirting. It may simply be the wrong moment, a mismatch in interest, or a person who would rather talk later—or not at all. Respecting that information is a skill, too.

15 Natural Ways to Flirt in Person (With Real Examples)

These ideas are not a script to run in order. Choose two or three that feel sincere and appropriate. Each tip includes what to do, a possible in-person example, how to read the moment and what to avoid.

1. Start with the moment you're both in

Rather than delivering a rehearsed line, mention the setting, the shared task or something you both just noticed. It gives the other person a clear, low-pressure way in.

In-person example

At a friend's game night: “I think we both had the same confused expression when the rules changed.”

What to do next: Pause. If they smile and add their own observation, explore it. If you get a brief answer, let them return to what they were doing.

Don't: Don't manufacture coincidences or interrupt someone who is busy, wearing headphones or trying to leave.

2. Use a warm, ordinary greeting

Simple confidence is often the willingness to say hello without demanding anything in return. A greeting gives you information about their willingness to engage.

In-person example

At a community class: “Hey, I'm Alex. Have you been to one of these before?”

What to do next: Introduce yourself at a normal volume, then give room for their response. You don't need a second sentence ready.

Don't: Don't treat a polite greeting as a romantic invitation.

3. Make eye contact briefly, then look away naturally

A glance can signal attention; staring can feel intrusive. Look at the person while listening, then look away as you naturally would with any comfortable conversation.

In-person example

During a relaxed conversation, meet their gaze when they make a point, smile gently if it feels natural, and look back at the shared activity.

What to do next: Notice whether the overall exchange feels easy—not whether someone holds your gaze for a particular number of seconds.

Don't: There is no universal eye-contact rule and no single glance that proves attraction.

4. Offer one specific, respectful compliment

Compliments feel less generic when they name a genuine choice, skill or quality rather than evaluating someone's body. They can be kind without creating an obligation.

In-person example

After they explain a complicated board game: “You make that much easier to understand. I like how patient you are.”

What to do next: Say it once, in a conversational tone, and move on. Let the compliment land without checking whether it worked.

Don't: Avoid sexualized comments, repeated appearance ratings or compliments that demand reassurance.

5. Ask an open question and actually use the answer

A light question opens a door; your response shows whether you're listening. Follow the detail they choose rather than pivoting through a memorized list.

In-person example

At a bookshop event: “What made you pick that author?” They say the dialogue. You reply, “Was there a character you kept thinking about?”

What to do next: Offer your view too, so they are not carrying the whole exchange.

Don't: Don't turn first contact into an interview or pressure them into personal disclosures.

6. Share a small piece of yourself

Gentle self-disclosure makes flirting feel like two people meeting, not one person performing. Choose something light and relevant before intimate stories.

In-person example

“I came because I promised myself I'd try one new thing this month. I'm still learning how not to overthink it.”

What to do next: Say something true that invites a response, then listen to what they choose to share.

Don't: Skip dramatic confessions or vulnerability that asks a stranger to comfort you.

7. Use playful humor that includes, not targets

A small shared joke can create ease when it grows from the same moment. Good teasing should never be a test or a put-down.

In-person example

After both of you lose the trivia round: “We may not win, but we're clearly the most creative wrong-answer team.”

What to do next: Laugh at the situation or yourself; see whether they join in and add their own joke.

Don't: Avoid negging, mocking appearance, personal insecurities or jokes that leave them defending themselves.

8. Let your posture say you're available, not entitled

Standing at a comfortable distance, keeping pathways open and giving full attention can feel inviting without becoming invasive.

In-person example

At a gallery opening, stand to the side rather than between them and the doorway. Turn toward them while they talk.

What to do next: Mirror the pace of the conversation rather than deliberately copying movements.

Don't: Do not block exits, corner someone, follow them across rooms or touch them to keep their attention.

9. Notice reciprocity across the conversation

Interest is easier to explore as a pattern of mutual participation than as a list of body-language signs. They may ask questions back, introduce topics or suggest continuing.

In-person example

You ask about the workshop; they ask why you came and later return to something you said.

What to do next: If they repeatedly offer fuller replies and initiate, you can try one slightly more personal invitation. Still don't assume romance.

Don't: A smile, politeness or eye contact alone cannot tell you someone's intentions.

10. Give the other person an easy out

Low-pressure flirting feels safer when saying no, ending the chat or changing the subject remains easy. This also prevents you from mistaking courtesy for enthusiasm.

In-person example

“I don't want to keep you from your friends. It was nice talking with you.”

What to do next: If they want to continue, they can choose to. If not, you've ended with dignity.

Don't: Never ask for an explanation after a clear or indirect refusal.

11. Be gently direct instead of endlessly hinting

Clear, friendly interest is often kinder than making someone interpret layers of signals. You can say you enjoy their company without demanding a reciprocal statement.

In-person example

“I'm enjoying talking with you. I'd like to get to know you better, if you're interested.”

What to do next: Say it once. Accept either an enthusiastic yes, a hesitant maybe or a no at face value.

Don't: Don't use declarations designed to make the other person feel indebted.

12. Match your energy to the setting

Flirting at a mutual friend's party is not the same as flirting with someone while they're working or receiving a service. Context can change whether an approach is welcome.

In-person example

At a casual social gathering: “Mind if I join this conversation?” At their workplace: stay courteous and don't interpret customer-service friendliness as interest.

What to do next: Choose contexts where both people have a genuine choice about participating.

Don't: Avoid romantic pressure in professional, instructional, medical or other unequal-power situations.

13. Use the smallest possible invitation to extend the conversation

If an exchange has been genuinely mutual, suggest something simple nearby or another shared activity rather than creating an elaborate demand.

In-person example

At an event: “I'm going to look at the next exhibit. Want to come along?”

What to do next: An invitation should be specific, optional and easy to decline.

Don't: If they say they're busy, don't repeatedly reschedule on their behalf.

14. Ask for a date once the interest seems mutual

You don't have to decode certainty before asking. A respectful invitation is a clearer way to learn whether someone wants to spend more time together.

In-person example

“I had a good time talking. Would you like to get coffee sometime next week? No pressure.”

What to do next: If they say yes, agree on a time and an accessible public place. If they decline, thank them and move on.

Don't: Don't interpret silence, nervous laughter or noncommittal answers as consent.

15. End at a good moment and respect the answer

The final impression should be that talking with you felt comfortable—not that they had to escape. Knowing when to stop is part of being charming.

In-person example

“I should head back to my friends, but it was lovely meeting you. Enjoy the rest of your night.”

What to do next: Keep your tone kind whether the interaction becomes a date, a friendship or a pleasant brief conversation.

Don't: After a no, disengage. Don't seek another route around the boundary.

How to Flirt in Real Life: Six Short Conversation Examples

Natural dialogue is usually brief. Notice the give-and-take rather than copying every word.

1. At a bookstore or library event

You: “Have you read anything else by this author?”
They: “Only one book. I liked the dialogue.”
You: “That might be what sold me too. What did you think of the ending?”

Why it works: A shared object starts a conversation; curiosity carries it forward. If they answer briefly and turn away, don't continue questioning.

2. At a friend's birthday party

You: “I think we're both part of the group that arrived before the food.”
They: “I planned my whole evening around those snacks.”
You: “Respectable strategy. How do you know Jess?”

Why it works: It's playful without teasing them personally. A friendly reply is not proof of romantic interest.

3. For a shy person at a recurring class

You: “I'm still figuring out this part. Did you find it easier the second time?”
They: “A little. This helped me.”
You: “That was useful. Thanks—I'm Alex, by the way.”

Why it works: You're joining a shared activity rather than announcing a crush. It is fine to leave it there for today.

4. When the conversation is going well

You: “It's been genuinely fun talking with you.”
They: “I was thinking the same thing.”
You: “Would you be interested in coffee sometime next week?”

Why it works: The invitation makes your intent clearer than an hour of guessing. Accept the answer without negotiating.

5. When they're not engaging

You: “How did you like the talk?”
They: “It was fine.” They turn back to their group.
You: “Glad you made it. Enjoy the rest of the event.”

Why it works: You're not punishing them for being unavailable, and you aren't looking for hidden encouragement.

6. When someone says no

You: “I'd like to see you again. Would you be interested?”
They: “Thanks, but no.”
You: “Thanks for being clear. Have a good evening.”

Why it works: Respect is attractive only in the ordinary sense of being decent; it is not a tactic to change their mind.

How to Flirt When You're Shy or Socially Awkward

If you tend to overthink conversations, use a one-sentence opening and a one-sentence follow-up. That is enough. You don't need to perform high energy or maintain eye contact throughout. An introverted person might be most comfortable at a regular class or gathering where there is a shared task; a social person might prefer a group conversation where they can make a direct invitation later. Both approaches can be authentic.

  • Choose a predictable setting: a book club, hobby night or a friend's gathering provides natural context.
  • Prepare a topic, not a personality: think of something about the event that genuinely interests you.
  • Practice pausing: a moment of silence is not a catastrophe or a cue to talk faster.
  • Stay curious rather than evaluating yourself: ask what you learned about them, not whether you looked perfect.
  • Honor your needs: if you prefer less eye contact, have sensory sensitivities, or communicate more directly, that is okay. You can say, “I'm a bit quiet when I first meet someone, but I've enjoyed this.”

Being shy is not a problem to erase. Persistent fear, panic or avoidance that causes significant distress may deserve support from a qualified professional, rather than aggressive “confidence tricks.” For related guidance, see how to make friends as an adult; the social confidence principles overlap, but the goals of friendship and romance are different.

How to Flirt With Body Language Without Being Creepy

Good flirting body language is usually ordinary respectful attention: standing at a comfortable distance, offering a genuine expression, looking toward the person while they speak, and not forcing contact. There is no scientifically reliable checklist of gestures that proves romantic interest.

Eye contact and smiling

A brief look and a natural smile can help make a greeting warm. Do not try to hold eye contact for an arbitrary number of seconds. Someone may look away because they are shy, neurodivergent, distracted or simply not interested; none of those explanations can be known from a single glance.

Distance, posture and touch

Keep an obvious path for the other person to leave. Never use touch as a “test” of attraction. Touch requires an appropriate context and consent; when uncertain, don't initiate it. In busy or constrained settings, even a friendly approach can feel intrusive if the person has no way to decline.

What positive signals do—and don't—mean

Someone who asks you questions, shares personal opinions, laughs with you and makes time to continue may be interested in talking. Those behaviors are not uniquely romantic. A clear invitation and voluntary follow-through are more informative than decoding crossed legs, pupil size or a smile. How to Flirt With a Guy, a Girl, or Someone of Any Gender

The basic approach is the same: respectful conversation, warmth, curiosity, clear intent and room for a no. There is no dependable rule that men prefer direct compliments while women prefer subtler clues, or vice versa. People vary more than those stereotypes imply.

If you're wondering how to flirt with a guy in person, focus on what he actually says and does rather than whether he's “supposed” to take the first step. If you're wondering how to flirt with a girl in person, don't assume friendliness is permission to escalate. If you're flirting with someone nonbinary, use the same respectful approach and don't make assumptions about identity, orientation or interest.

Directness is compatible with tenderness: “I like talking with you” is simpler and more honest than trying to trigger jealousy, confusion or competitive attention. If it feels relevant, ask about a person's preferred name or pronouns with everyday courtesy—not as a performance.

Where Flirting Works—and Where to Be Especially Careful

At a bar, party or social event

Start in a normal social area, check their willingness to talk and let the other person return to their group. Do not use alcohol to reduce someone's ability to say no; intoxication makes judgment and consent harder, not easier.

At a gym or exercise class

Most people are there to exercise. Don't interrupt sets, workouts or headphone use. A brief conversation during an appropriate social break may be fine, but accept signs they want to focus on training.

At work, or when someone is serving you

Courtesy, eye contact and friendly conversation may be part of the job. Avoid asking for contact details or a date when the person is working, and never use professional authority as leverage. If a colleague is involved, respect workplace rules and potential power imbalances.

At school, college or a shared activity

A short exchange after a class or at a voluntary gathering can be more appropriate than interrupting a lesson or pressuring a classmate. Keep invitations optional and don't let a refusal become gossip or retaliation.

With someone you already know

Moving from friendship to romantic interest can be delicate because the relationship already matters. Use plain language, offer a genuine choice, and be prepared for the friendship to stay a friendship. For more about early romantic uncertainty, see our talking stage guide.

How to Turn Flirting Into a Date Without Making It Weird

After a comfortable, mutual exchange, you can choose clarity over analysis. Ask once, make the invitation specific and allow for an easy no. Examples:

After a shared event: “I'd like to talk more. Would you be interested in coffee next weekend?”

After a group conversation: “I've enjoyed getting to know you. Would you like to go to that free exhibit together sometime?”

If they seem unsure: “No worries at all. It was nice meeting you.”

Don't insist on exchanging numbers, demand an immediate decision or make a “joke” of an uncomfortable refusal. If someone gives an enthusiastic yes, suggest a public, accessible location and confirm plans clearly. Our 35 cheap first date ideas can help once there's mutual interest.

10 Flirting Mistakes That Make Things Awkward

  1. Using a canned pickup line unrelated to the moment: it can sound like a performance.
  2. Talking through every pause: leave room for both people to participate.
  3. Complimenting appearance repeatedly: one genuine, respectful observation is enough.
  4. Confusing a smile with consent: friendliness is not proof of attraction.
  5. Inventing rules for eye contact: natural attention is better than staring.
  6. Touching to “test chemistry”: don't treat someone else's body as a signal generator.
  7. Trying to make them jealous: manipulation is not connection.
  8. Pursuing someone who is working or cannot leave freely: context and power matter.
  9. Ignoring a brief answer or refusal: one no is enough.
  10. Overanalyzing your performance afterward: focus on reciprocity and how respectfully you behaved.

What Does Science Say About Flirting?

Flirting research offers useful explanations for why people signal interest differently and why interpretations can be unreliable. It does not provide a guaranteed sequence of moves that will make another person attracted to you.

The five flirting styles

Communication researcher Jeffrey A. Hall and colleagues described five broad flirting styles—physical, traditional, polite, sincere and playful—in peer-reviewed work. These are research categories for reported behavior and preferences, not medical diagnoses or boxes people must fit into. A person may be direct in one setting and reserved in another. It's a reminder that one-size-fits-all advice misses individual variation.

People are not reliable mind readers

Hall and colleagues also studied how accurately people recognize flirting in brief interactions. Participants often had difficulty distinguishing flirting from friendly behavior. Public retellings sometimes reduce the finding to a single percentage, but that can obscure differences in groups and study conditions. The practical lesson is narrower: don't treat one smile, laugh or glance as definitive evidence of romantic interest. Mutual conversation and a clear invitation are more useful than a secret body-language code.

Research can't replace the other person's choice

Studies about typical behavior don't determine what this particular person feels. Cultural norms, personality, neurodivergence, orientation, context and safety can all influence how people communicate. A respectful, honest conversation is always preferable to testing a theory on someone who is not comfortable.

How to Flirt: Frequently Asked Questions

How do you flirt with someone in person?

Start with a friendly greeting or a genuine observation about the setting. Listen, ask one open question, share your perspective and look for mutual engagement. If the conversation feels comfortable, make one clear, low-pressure invitation.

How can I flirt without being awkward?

Use a small opening connected to where you are, speak naturally and allow pauses. Focus on learning about the other person, not performing a perfect line. If they are not engaged, end politely rather than trying harder.

How do I flirt with my crush face-to-face?

Choose a situation where talking is welcome, such as a shared class or group event. Say hello, mention something you're both experiencing and let the exchange build only if they participate. You can be direct about wanting to see them again.

How do you flirt if you're shy?

Try a one-sentence observation followed by one simple question. A quieter style is completely valid. You do not need intense eye contact, loud humor or touch to show interest.

How can you tell if flirting is working?

Notice whether the other person voluntarily participates, asks questions and chooses to continue. None of these alone proves attraction. A clear, pressure-free invitation is a more reliable way to learn if they want a date.

What is good flirting body language?

Relaxed posture, an appropriate distance, a natural smile and attention while listening may make a conversation warmer. Do not block exits or use touch to test interest. There is no universal gesture that proves attraction.

How do I flirt with a guy in person?

Treat him as an individual: start with shared context, offer a sincere compliment, listen and be direct if you want to ask him on a date. Avoid assumptions that he must make the first move.

How do I flirt with a girl in person?

Start respectfully, make conversation easy to opt out of, ask thoughtful questions and do not interpret friendliness as romantic interest. If the exchange is mutual, ask once whether she would like to meet again.

Is teasing a good way to flirt?

Playful humor can work when both people are enjoying it, but put-downs, appearance jokes, negging and public embarrassment are not respectful flirting. Humor about a shared situation is a safer choice.

How do I stop flirting when the other person isn't interested?

Keep it simple: say something like “Nice talking with you—enjoy the event,” then give them space. Do not ask for reasons, follow them or repeatedly try new approaches.

Can I flirt with someone who is working?

It is usually better not to make romantic advances when someone is required to be polite or cannot leave freely. Professional friendliness is not a signal of attraction. Always respect workplace rules and power differences.

What is the difference between flirting and friendliness?

They overlap. A smile, a joke or eye contact may be ordinary friendliness. Sustained mutual participation and a clearly welcomed invitation provide more information, but you cannot determine another person's feelings with certainty from behavior alone.
