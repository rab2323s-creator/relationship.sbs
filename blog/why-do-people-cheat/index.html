Why Do People Cheat? 8 Psychological Reasons Research Explains

Why would someone cheat on a person they love—or when the relationship seems happy? Studies reveal motives beyond dissatisfaction: validation, resentment, low commitment, opportunity, and more. Here is what the evidence really says.

October 12, 202616 min readResearch-informed editorial guide

On Thursday evenings, Daniel carried two cups of tea into the living room. He always gave Eva the blue one because the handle on the white cup had chipped years before. They had the familiar intimacy of people who knew which light to leave on in the hall. Their friends called them happy. They sometimes called themselves happy, too. And somewhere between that blue cup and the next morning's train, Daniel had begun keeping a secret that would eventually change the meaning of every ordinary kindness.

When Eva learned about the affair, she asked a question that felt almost mathematical: What was missing? Daniel had answers, then contradictory answers, then silence. He spoke about feeling invisible at work. He spoke about excitement. He said he loved her. None of it added up to the single cause Eva was desperate to find. She wanted the explanation that would make the betrayal understandable—and, perhaps, impossible to happen again.

That is the cruel difficulty of the question why do people cheat? A reason can make an action intelligible without making it justified. And sometimes the person who cheated does not fully understand their own motives, either.

Daniel and Eva are fictional characters. This scene illustrates the question, not any particular research participant.

Why do people cheat? The research-based answer

Infidelity can have several motives at once: feeling neglected, wanting a different sexual connection, falling out of love, low commitment, seeking validation, wanting novelty, anger or retaliation, and situational pressures or opportunities. In a study of 495 people who reported cheating, researchers identified eight distinct motivation factors. Some involve problems within a relationship; others concern the individual's desires or circumstances. No motive removes responsibility for breaking an agreement. Read the original study.

The psychology of cheating: why a single explanation fails

Popular advice often divides cheaters into two neat groups: unhappy people seeking what their relationship lacks, and selfish people who simply want more. Real behavior is less orderly. A person may feel both attached and dissatisfied. Someone else may feel loved but crave approval from strangers. A third person may describe an impulsive opportunity while also carrying a long-standing pattern of poor boundaries.

That is why psychological research distinguishes motives from risk factors and choices. A motive is what someone says they were pursuing. A risk factor is something statistically associated with a greater chance of the behavior across groups. A choice is what the person actually did—and still owns.

Three different questions people often confuse

  • Motive: What was the person seeking, escaping, or expressing?
  • Context: What made cheating more available or tempting at that time?
  • Responsibility: What did the person choose instead of keeping or honestly renegotiating an agreement?

Understanding the first two can inform a conversation. Neither answers the third for someone.

This separation also protects the betrayed partner from a misleading conclusion: “If the cause was neglect, I must have caused the cheating.” The 2019 motivation study did not establish that partners' shortcomings caused affairs. Participants were describing their own reasons after the fact. Their experiences deserve analysis, not automatic endorsement.

8 psychological reasons people cheat, according to research

The most useful starting point is Selterman, Garcia and Tsapelas's peer-reviewed study, published in The Journal of Sex Research in 2019. The researchers surveyed 495 people, most of them younger adults recruited through an online questionnaire, who reported having been unfaithful. Their work found eight distinguishable self-reported motives. These are categories of explanation, not eight diagnoses or a ranking of the most common causes in the population.

01 / 08

1. Feeling neglected or emotionally unseen

Some people describe an accumulation of moments in which they no longer feel wanted, listened to, or important. An outside connection can begin to feel like a room where someone finally notices the parts of them that have gone quiet at home. Researchers distinguish this neglect motive from simply wanting another partner or wanting more variety.

But feeling neglected does not create an obligation for a partner to provide unlimited attention, and it does not remove the choice to be honest, ask for changes, seek help, or end a relationship before crossing an agreement. The relevant question is not whether the complaint was imaginary. It is why a real or perceived unmet need was handled through secrecy rather than discussion.

Psychological distinction: “I felt alone” describes an experience. “Therefore I had no choice” is a justification, not a finding from research.

02 / 08

2. A mismatch in sexual desire or expectations

Some people report wanting a different level or kind of sexual connection than their relationship currently offers. In the motivation research, sexual desire forms a distinct factor rather than being treated as the universal cause of infidelity. That distinction matters: not all affairs happen because a couple is unhappy, and not all unhappy couples have affairs.

Difference in desire can be a real relationship challenge. It is not consent to break a commitment. Partners may negotiate expectations, consider support, or decide they are incompatible; they are still responsible for how they respond to the difference. If your question is about your own change in interest, our Why Don't I Want Sex With My Partner? quiz addresses a separate issue without declaring anyone unfaithful.

03 / 08

3. Feeling less in love or romantically attached

A person may experience the relationship as increasingly companionable or emotionally finished while remaining physically present in it. In Selterman and colleagues' study, lack of love emerged as its own reported motive, separate from anger, neglect, and desire for novelty.

Here is the painful paradox: someone can remain in a relationship for shared history, children, money, uncertainty, or fear of hurting another person, while internally treating the romance as over. None of those reasons automatically causes cheating. The ethical alternative is difficult honesty about what has changed. If the central question is attraction rather than infidelity, the partner attraction reflection quiz explores that without reducing the answer to a yes-or-no judgment.

04 / 08

4. Low commitment or unclear agreement about exclusivity

Commitment is more than an affectionate sentence. It includes whether someone accepts a shared agreement as binding when an appealing alternative appears. The research identifies low commitment as a distinct motive: some people were less invested in their primary relationship, or understood exclusivity differently from their partner.

There is an important difference between an agreement that was genuinely never made and an agreement that was made but later treated as optional. Couples can have consensually non-monogamous arrangements; that is not automatically infidelity. Cheating concerns deception or violating the rules of the relationship that actually exists. “I wasn't sure we were exclusive” calls for examining what was communicated—not automatically accepting or rejecting the explanation.

05 / 08

5. Wanting validation, attention or a sense of being desirable

Sometimes the hidden reward is not primarily a new relationship. It is a new image of the self: exciting, admired, wanted, no longer invisible or ordinary. The research calls this an esteem motivation. A person might be searching for confirmation of their attractiveness or independence rather than responding to a specific failing in their partner.

This does not mean everyone who cheats has “low self-esteem,” or that the behavior proves narcissism. The study measured self-reported motives, not a clinical diagnosis. The useful psychological question is: What did the attention allow this person to believe about themselves? If a person needs outside admiration to feel worthwhile, repairing a relationship alone may not address that vulnerability.

06 / 08

6. Seeking novelty, variety or a different experience

Curiosity, novelty and the wish for different experiences were another motive. The desire for variety is not identical to dissatisfaction with an existing partner; in the research it was identifiable separately. That is one reason the simple explanation “they must have been unhappy at home” is inadequate.

Being interested in novelty is a feeling, not an instruction. People make choices about what to do with that interest. Couples can discuss compatible expectations, negotiate a mutually wanted relationship structure, or separate honestly. Secrecy is the element that turns exploration into a betrayal of shared terms.

07 / 08

7. Anger, resentment or retaliation

An affair can sometimes function as retaliation: an attempt to restore a sense of power, express anger, or inflict pain after a grievance. Barta and Kiene studied anger as a motive in 2005; the later eight-factor model also distinguished it from neglect and lack of love.

This is not evidence that the person who was betrayed “drove” the other person to cheat. Anger can be real and still be handled in destructive ways. When cheating is used to punish, the relationship may have a wider problem with accountability, conflict, or attempts to control the emotional balance between partners. A revenge narrative tells you something about the person's explanation; it does not determine whether that explanation is fair.

08 / 08

8. Opportunity, stress, intoxication or a particular setting

Some participants attributed an affair to the surrounding circumstances: travel, unusually high stress, substance use, a social setting, or another period when they felt less deliberate. Researchers grouped these as situational factors rather than assuming every affair reflected an unhappy attachment.

Context can change risk and decision-making, but it should not be used as a universal excuse. “I was drinking” does not answer what boundaries were crossed, what precautions a person ordinarily takes, or whether they are now being truthful. And importantly, intoxication does not by itself establish what happened or whether all parties could consent; where consent is in question, avoid simplistic labels and seek appropriate support.

Why do people cheat in happy relationships?

The phrase happy relationship is not a laboratory measurement. It might mean two people rarely argue, share an affectionate home, have satisfying routines, or would have rated their relationship positively before a betrayal. Those are different observations. And the person who cheated may not experience the relationship in exactly the same way as the partner who was hurt.

Still, the question is legitimate. The eight-motive framework helps explain how cheating can happen without an obvious relationship crisis. Esteem, variety, commitment and situational motives are not simply other names for “my partner failed to meet my needs.” A person can enjoy their partner's companionship while seeking outside validation; they can love the stability of a relationship while struggling to honor its agreement.

That does not prove happy couples are just as likely to experience infidelity as unhappy couples; the study was not designed to establish that comparison. It means the absence of visible conflict cannot exclude the possibility of cheating—and that an affair does not automatically tell the full story of the relationship's prior quality.

One practical lesson: if someone claims they cheated because the relationship was secretly miserable, it is reasonable to ask what they actually said or did about that unhappiness beforehand. An explanation that appeared only after discovery should be examined, not treated as unquestionable proof.

Why do people cheat on someone they love?

Love is a feeling and a commitment can be a set of behaviors. They often strengthen one another, but they are not identical. Some people who cheat say they still loved their partner. That may describe a genuine feeling, a mixed feeling, a wish to avoid loss, or an attempt to reduce the consequences of disclosure. No outside observer can verify love from a single statement.

It helps to replace “If they loved me, this could not have happened” with a more useful question: Whatever they felt, did they protect the agreements they chose? Love does not make trustworthiness automatic. It is also possible to care for someone and decide that the relationship no longer fits; honesty remains an available path.

For the partner who was betrayed, this matters because the betrayal does not prove they were insufficiently interesting, attractive, attentive, or deserving. A person's reported motive is information about their choices, not a global judgment about another person's worth.

Does attachment style explain cheating?

Attachment patterns are one lens on how people manage closeness, reassurance, and independence. They are not a lie detector and cannot identify who will cheat. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis gathered 17 studies with 13,666 participants. Across the relevant measures, attachment insecurity was associated with marital infidelity at about r = 0.18—a statistically detectable but relatively modest association.

Someone with anxiety about rejection may seek reassurance in unhealthy ways. Someone who becomes uncomfortable with closeness may withdraw or compartmentalize. But the research does not show that either pattern inevitably leads to an affair, and neither should be used to diagnose a partner from an accusation. Most importantly, correlation is not causation; other factors, measurement methods, and relationship context may be involved.

If you're learning about these patterns, read what attachment styles mean or take the attachment style reflection test—not as a way to predict betrayal, but to understand the wider vocabulary of closeness and boundaries.

Do different cheating motives lead to different kinds of affairs?

Possibly—and this is where the evidence becomes more interesting than a simple list. In a 2021 analysis by Selterman, Garcia and Tsapelas, researchers connected reported motives with what participants said happened during and after their affairs. Relationship-focused motives, such as anger or lack of love, were associated with longer affairs, more public dates, and dissolution of the primary relationship. Situational motives were associated with shorter affairs and lower rates of disclosure and dissolution.

Those findings are associations in a self-report sample. They are not a formula for predicting the duration of a specific affair. The analysis drew on the same 495-person research program as the eight-motive paper, so it should not be presented as an independent replication. Nor does the finding imply that a short affair causes less pain, or that circumstances make one form of betrayal less serious.

The broader implication is that the motive someone reports can change the questions worth asking. “What were you seeking?” is different from “Why did you keep a second relationship going?” The answers may matter for honesty and future decisions, but responsibility is not diluted by the category.

Study design matters. The eight-motive survey asked people to explain their own past behavior; it did not experimentally manipulate motives or observe an entire population. The meta-analysis summarizes observational associations, and the longitudinal study observes risk across relationships without declaring any individual's future.

What the 2005 study contributed

Barta and Kiene found four motive dimensions in a study of college students: dissatisfaction, neglect, anger and sexual motives. This early work challenged the idea that all cheating could be reduced to one emotional reason or one sexual reason. Its student sample and historic context limit how confidently it can describe all couples today.

What previous cheating says about future behavior

The 2017 longitudinal study by Knopp and colleagues followed 484 participants across two relationships. People who reported sexual infidelity in the first were roughly three times as likely to report it in the next, compared with people who did not report infidelity the first time. That is a relative risk comparison in a particular sample, not a 100% prediction. The authors explicitly noted that most people with the relevant experience in the first relationship did not report the same experience again during the next one.

The honest conclusion is uncomfortable precisely because it refuses both slogans: past cheating can matter, and change remains possible. Neither “everyone repeats” nor “a good apology resets the risk” is supported by the study.

Why do men cheat, and why do women cheat? What the evidence can—and can't—say

Search results often promise two tidy lists: men cheat for one reason, women for another. Older studies and the 495-person survey found some differences in reported motives across their samples. But those findings depend on who participated, how cheating was defined, and how questions were asked. They do not establish immutable male and female psychologies.

A person's willingness to speak honestly, respect commitments, and manage boundaries is not determined by gender. Social expectations can also influence which motives people admit. A better question for any individual is: What choices did this particular person make, and what evidence supports their explanation?

How to understand someone's reason for cheating without excusing it

When people say “I just needed to feel alive” or “It meant nothing,” they may be trying to describe something real in language that protects them from the full weight of what they did. The phrase can contain both insight and evasion. It is worth listening to the parts that describe concrete experience while asking whether the person is accepting responsibility.

Use this four-part reality check

  1. Name the motive. Was the person seeking attention, revenge, novelty, reassurance, or escape from a relationship they had stopped investing in?
  2. Look for the actual choice. Which agreement was crossed? Was there secrecy, repeated contact, or an opportunity to stop?
  3. Listen for accountability. Can the person say what they chose without making you responsible for it?
  4. Compare the story with behavior. A persuasive explanation is not the same as honesty, repaired boundaries, or consistent change.

This is also why amateur diagnosis is usually a detour. It may feel satisfying to label a cheating partner narcissistic, avoidant, addicted to excitement, or incapable of love. But motive studies do not provide a diagnosis of that person. Their behavior can be unacceptable without being explained by a disorder.

If you're uncertain whether an observed behavior even crosses the relationship's agreement, begin with emotional cheating versus friendship or the micro-cheating examples guide. This article answers a different question: not “what counts?” but “what might motivate the choice?”

If your partner cheated, which questions are worth asking?

You do not have to become a psychologist to understand what happened. And you do not owe someone a chance to repair things because their explanation sounds sophisticated. If it is safe and you want a conversation, a few specific questions may offer more clarity than hours of circular argument:

  1. “What were you looking for that you weren't addressing honestly?”
  2. “When did you understand that this crossed our agreement?”
  3. “Which part of the explanation is context, and which part is a choice you take responsibility for?”
  4. “What would you do differently before reaching the same situation again?”
  5. “What specific actions, rather than promises, would demonstrate change?”

These questions aren't a script for extracting a confession. They are an invitation to examine the gap between motive and accountability. If the answer is defensiveness, threats, or pressure for immediate forgiveness, prioritize your wellbeing. For more practical information, see actions that build trust over time and how to distinguish mistrust from evidence.

Five things the psychology of cheating does not prove

  1. That an affair proves the betrayed partner failed. A participant's feeling of neglect is not a causal study of the other person's behavior.
  2. That everyone who cheats has a personality disorder. The motives are descriptive categories, not clinical diagnostic criteria.
  3. That a happy relationship makes cheating impossible. Some reported motives are not direct measures of relationship dissatisfaction.
  4. That opportunity excuses infidelity. Situational context may explain how a decision became available without making the breach acceptable.
  5. That a past affair determines a person's future. The longitudinal evidence identifies elevated group risk, not destiny for an individual.

Frequently asked questions about why people cheat

What is the biggest psychological reason people cheat?

There is no single proven biggest cause across all people. Research identifies several distinguishable self-reported motives, including neglect, anger, desire, lack of love, low commitment, self-esteem, variety and situational factors. The 495-person motivation study was not a representative ranking of the entire population.

Why do people cheat even if they are in a happy relationship?

Some motives concern validation, novelty, low commitment, or the situation rather than an explicit complaint about the partner. But outside observers cannot always know how satisfied each partner felt, and the research cannot determine a particular person's motive without their own honest account.

Why would someone cheat on a person they love?

Feelings and decisions are different. Someone may experience affection or attachment and still break a shared agreement. A claim of love does not erase the betrayal or demonstrate that the relationship is safe to continue.

Is cheating always caused by unmet needs?

No. Feeling neglected or dissatisfied can be one reported motive, but studies also find low commitment, anger, esteem, variety and situational motivations. A person's needs do not remove their responsibility for what they chose to do.

Does childhood trauma or avoidant attachment make someone cheat?

No attachment style or difficult childhood guarantees infidelity. A 2024 meta-analysis found a modest association between attachment insecurity and marital infidelity across studies, not a direct causal mechanism for an individual person.

Is cheating a sign of low self-esteem?

Seeking affirmation or a more desirable self-image can be one motivation, but not everyone who cheats reports it. An esteem motive should not be mistaken for a diagnosis or applied automatically to people who are unfaithful.

Can someone cheat once and never cheat again?

Yes, it is possible. A longitudinal study found higher average risk of cheating in a later relationship among people who had reported it before, but most with that prior experience did not report the same behavior again in the later relationship during the study period.

Do men and women cheat for different reasons?

Some surveys have found differences in reported motives, but findings vary with samples, time and definitions. Gender alone cannot reveal why a particular person cheated.

Is emotional cheating psychologically different from a physical affair?

It can involve different boundaries and experiences, including secrecy, displaced closeness and romantic interest. Definitions depend partly on a couple's agreement. Our emotional cheating guide addresses the distinction directly.

Does understanding why someone cheated mean I should forgive them?

No. Understanding a motive, considering forgiveness, rebuilding trust and choosing to stay are separate decisions. You may want an explanation without offering reconciliation.

Research references, methods and editorial scope

The eight categories above come from one influential exploratory research program, not from an experiment proving eight universal causes. Because infidelity is defined differently across studies and participants self-report sensitive behavior, findings should be applied cautiously. This guide is educational and does not diagnose an individual or tell a reader whether to remain in a relationship. The opening characters are fictional.

The hardest thing about betrayal is that an explanation can arrive without restoring what was broken. Psychological research can make the motives less mysterious. It cannot make an act harmless, and it cannot ask someone else to carry the responsibility for it. The most important evidence, after the reason has been spoken, is what a person chooses to do next.
