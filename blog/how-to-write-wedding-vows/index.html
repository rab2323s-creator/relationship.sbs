How to Write Wedding Vows in 7 Steps (Even if You Are Not a Writer)

How to write wedding vows: choose one true memory, name the quality it reveals, write three promises you can practice, acknowledge that life will change, and finish with a clear commitment. Keep the language conversational, read it aloud, and check with your officiant whether your personal vows are separate from required legal wording.

Good vows do not have to explain the entire history of your relationship. They have to let your partner hear something unmistakably yours. If you are staring at a blank page, do not begin with the phrase “I promise.” Begin with a scene: the kitchen after a difficult day, a bus stop where someone waited in the rain, the moment your partner quietly noticed what you needed without being asked.

  1. Choose the feeling you want to leave behind. Steady, grateful, hopeful, playful, or quietly serious? You can be two things at once.
  2. Write one ordinary, detailed memory. Choose a small scene that shows who your partner is; avoid a private joke that only two people can understand without any context.
  3. Name the value beneath the memory. Perhaps it showed you patience, curiosity, courage, kindness, room to change, or the ability to disagree without leaving each other alone.
  4. Make three distinct promises. One for everyday life, one for difficult moments, and one for the kind of future you want to practice together.
  5. Include a human limit and a repair promise. Instead of guaranteeing you will never be impatient, promise to notice, apologize and try again.
  6. Close with a choice. A line such as “I choose to build this life with you, one day at a time” communicates intention without borrowing a grand quotation.
  7. Read and trim. Speak slowly with room for tears, laughter, and a breath. Replace stiff phrases with words you actually use.

A simple first draft can be imperfect. It only needs a truthful beginning. Once you have words on the page, the private wedding vow writer below helps you shape them into a draft that belongs to you.

Wedding Vow Template: A Fill-in-the-Blank Structure You Can Personalize

Wedding vow template: “[Name], when I remember [specific moment], I see [quality] in you. You have taught me [what changed]. I promise to [everyday action], to [action in hard times], and to [future commitment]. When I get it wrong, I will [repair action]. I choose [shared future], with you.”

Opening: “[Name], the moment I keep coming back to is [one real scene].”

Meaning: “It showed me [specific quality] and taught me [what that quality makes possible].”

Promises: “I promise to [a small repeatable behavior], [a hard-day behavior], and [a shared future behavior].”

Repair: “When we misunderstand each other, I will [what you will do, not what you hope they will do].”

Ending: “I choose [the life you are making] with you, again and again.”

You can use this structure for non-religious wedding vows, a second marriage, an intimate ceremony, or a large wedding. It is intentionally gender-neutral and free of copied ceremonial language. Change every word that sounds like somebody else's relationship. A useful wedding vow outline gives you direction; it should never erase your voice.

How many promises should be in wedding vows?

Three concrete promises make a readable starting point: everyday care, conflict or difficulty, and a shared future. Professional vow writers interviewed by Vogue often suggest several specific commitments, but there is no universal required number. Three is an editorial structure here, not a research-backed formula or a legal requirement.

The Yellow Grocery List: A Short Original Story About Wedding Vows

The most moving wedding vows sometimes begin with the least ceremonial evidence of love: an unglamorous promise kept on a Tuesday. A small remembered action can make a vow believable long after the music ends.

The night before her wedding, Ellie had written “You are my sun, my moon, my everything” four different ways. She crossed out all four. None sounded like her.

Under a stack of menus she found an old yellow grocery list. Between onions and coffee, there was a line in Max's untidy handwriting: “Ask Ellie how the meeting went.” She remembered that week. She had been rehearsing a difficult conversation at work. Max couldn't fix the meeting; he wasn't supposed to. He simply remembered she would need someone to listen afterward.

At the ceremony, Ellie folded the grocery list inside her vow booklet. “You remember the small things,” she said, looking at Max rather than the guests. “So I promise to make room for your small things, too. I won't always know how to help. I'll ask. I'll listen. If I rush to solve something you only wanted me to hear, I'll stop and begin again.”

She did not promise never to be tired or frustrated. She promised what she would do when she was. Afterward, their friends remembered the yellow list more clearly than any grand declaration. Max kept it in a drawer, next to the extra pens. There were still groceries to buy on Monday.

Original fictional editorial scene. Ellie and Max are invented characters; this is not a reported interview, a client case, or a claim that one vow style predicts marriage outcomes.

Our Promise-to-Practice Method: Write Wedding Vows That Mean Something After the Ceremony

The Promise-to-Practice Method is our original four-part editorial tool: memory → value → behavior → repair. Start with something your partner actually did, identify what it meant to you, promise a realistic action in response, and name how you will return to that promise when you fall short.

This is a writing method developed by relationship.sbs, not a clinically validated marriage intervention. Its research inspiration is the difference between making a big promise and having the skills and plan to keep one.

What the psychology actually says about keeping promises

In four studies reported in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Peetz and Kammrath (2011) found that people focused on positive relationship feelings or responding to their partner often made larger promises, but were not necessarily better at keeping them. Self-regulation and thinking through how to follow through were more useful predictors or interventions in those study settings. The research examined everyday romantic promises, not wedding vows as a ceremony, so it does not show that a certain vow format will improve a marriage. Read the study abstract and the university explanation of its findings.

What we take from the evidence: write a promise that can survive a stressful Thursday, not only sound beautiful on a sunny Saturday. That's an editorial application of the research—not a proven formula for relationship success.

How Long Should Wedding Vows Be? 60, 90 and 120-Second Guide

Most personal wedding vows can fit into roughly one to two minutes if that suits your ceremony. A 60-second vow might contain about 105–125 spoken words, 90 seconds about 160–185, and 120 seconds about 215–250 at a measured pace of roughly 110–125 words per minute. These are planning estimates, not formal rules; pauses and emotion add time.

How do I estimate reading time for my wedding vows?

Count words, divide by the speaking rate in words per minute, and multiply by 60. Estimated seconds = words ÷ pace × 60. At 125 words per minute, 180 words take about 86 seconds before pauses. Our editor adds a small pause allowance and reports an estimate, not a recording. A real rehearsal with a stopwatch is more accurate.

Short Wedding Vows: Three Original Examples That Still Feel Personal

Short wedding vows work when they include one distinctive truth and specific promises rather than generic superlatives. You can say something meaningful in under a minute. The examples below are original drafts for inspiration; adapt their situations and wording so they become true for you.

Short wedding vows — simple and sincere

“Morgan, I love the way you make room for both joy and quiet. When the week gets loud, you are the person I want to tell about it, and the person I want to sit beside afterward. I promise to listen when your day is heavy, to share the unglamorous work of a home, and to keep learning who you are as we change. I will not always get everything right. I will apologize when I don't, and I will make time to try again. I choose you, and the ordinary life we will build together.”

Short wedding vows for her — personal, not possessive

“Avery, the first thing I admired was your curiosity. The thing I trust now is your honesty. You taught me that love can be both gentle and brave. I promise to ask how you're doing and wait for the real answer. I promise to cheer for the dreams that are yours alone, not only the plans we share. And I promise that when we disagree, I will stay respectful and come back to the conversation. I'm proud to call you my partner. Today I choose the life ahead of us with you.”

Short wedding vows for him — warm and grounded

“Jamie, I knew I loved being with you when a missed train became an hour of laughing instead of an hour of frustration. You make the inconvenient parts of life easier to face. I promise to be your teammate when plans change, to notice the effort nobody else sees, and to bring a little lightness into our hard weeks. I won't always know what to say. I will keep asking and listening. I love you, and I choose to make this life with you—with all its detours.”

All sample names and scenarios are invented. The “for her” and “for him” examples are simply alternate phrasings; every vow can be adapted for any gender or relationship.

Personal Wedding Vows Examples: A 90-Second and a Two-Minute Draft

Personal wedding vows examples are most useful when they show a relationship-specific memory and commitments you could realistically act on. These two original examples illustrate a shorter, story-led version and a longer, reflective one. They're models of structure, not scripts you need to copy word for word.

Example 1: Personal wedding vows with a memory and three promises (about 90 seconds)

“Rowan, I think of the evening we tried to assemble that bookshelf. We got the shelves backward, misplaced the screws, and ended up sitting on the floor with pizza. You laughed, found the instructions, and asked what I thought we should do next. That night told me something important: with you, a mistake can become a problem we solve together, not a reason to blame each other. I admire your patience and your willingness to start again. I promise to bring that same patience when plans change. I promise to listen all the way through, especially when an answer is hard to hear. And I promise to protect time for our shared adventures as well as the quiet work of home. When I get something wrong, I will admit it and help put it right. I love the life we are learning to make, and I choose to keep making it with you.”

Reading time depends on your delivery. This illustrative script is not guaranteed to take exactly 90 seconds; count and rehearse it at your own speaking pace.

Example 2: Heartfelt wedding vows for a longer ceremony (about two minutes)

“Alex, when people ask when I knew, I usually tell them about our trip to the mountains. But the truth is quieter. I knew when you sat beside me on a difficult Tuesday and let me be silent. You did not turn my bad day into a project. You made tea, handed me the warm mug, and stayed. You have shown me that love isn't only the stories we tell after a wonderful weekend. It is the attention we give each other on the days that don't make good photographs. I love your way of noticing people. I love that you ask another question when the first answer is too easy. I love that you keep learning even when learning means admitting you were wrong. So today I make promises I want to practice, not simply speak. I will ask what you need instead of assuming I know. I will make our home a place where your dreams are welcome, including the ones I don't fully understand yet. I will share the work of an ordinary life and not call it help when it is ours to carry. When I am tired, I will try to be kind. When I am wrong, I will apologize and act differently. And when the years change us both, I will stay curious about the person you are becoming. I cannot promise us a life without loss or conflict. I can promise to meet that life honestly, with you, as your partner. Today I choose our unfolding story.”

To make a borrowed example genuinely yours, replace the memory first, then the promised behaviors. Don't change only the name. A sentence grounded in a shared fact is more personal than a perfectly polished generic declaration.

Non-Religious Wedding Vows: Secular Templates and Original Examples

Non-religious wedding vows can center on mutual respect, care, choice, and the life two people are building without invoking a deity or religious text. A simple secular format is: a true story, what you value in your partner, specific promises, and a freely chosen commitment. Check any venue or registry restrictions separately.

Short non-religious wedding vows example

“Taylor, I choose you freely, as the person you are today and the person you are still becoming. I promise to treat your hopes as seriously as my own, to tell you the truth with care, and to do my share of the daily work that makes a home. When we disagree, I will remember that the problem is something for us to face, not a reason to make you my opponent. I can't promise perfect days. I can promise attention, effort, and the willingness to repair when I fall short. I am grateful to begin this next chapter beside you.”

Modern non-religious wedding vow template

“[Name], I admire [one real quality] and see it in [one brief moment]. I want to build [shared kind of life], without asking either of us to stop growing. I promise to [everyday care], [honest conflict behavior], and [shared-future action]. When I make a mistake, I will [repair]. I choose to be your partner, beginning today.”

Secular and civil are not interchangeable. A non-religious personal promise may be fine for a symbolic ceremony but might not replace the mandatory declaration in a legally recognized marriage. Official rules in England and Wales, Australia, and Germany differ; see our ceremony-language comparison.

Wedding Vows for a Second Marriage: Respectful Promises for a New Chapter

Wedding vows for a second marriage should honor the relationship being chosen now, without treating a previous marriage as a mistake that had to happen or promising that history will disappear. Speak about what you value today, the commitments you can make, and any shared family responsibilities you have agreed to carry.

Second marriages come from many different experiences: divorce, widowhood, later-life partnership, blended families, or people whose paths simply met at another stage of life. Avoid public details about former spouses, past pain, custody or children unless everyone involved welcomes them. Your vow is addressed to your partner, not an explanation to the audience.

Original heartfelt vows for a second marriage

“Casey, we didn't meet at the beginning of our stories. We met as two people with full lives, loved ones, responsibilities, and hopes that were still unfolding. I love the care you bring to this chapter without asking me to erase what came before. I promise to be honest about what I can offer, to protect the time we make for each other, and to keep room for the people who matter to us both. I will listen when the past makes something harder, and I will not ask you to pretend it never happened. I choose this life with you deliberately, tenderly, and with the courage to begin again.”

Wedding vows for a second marriage with children

Do not automatically vow to become a child's new parent or promise affection on behalf of children. Instead, if applicable and agreed, you can say: “I will respect the bonds that already exist in our families, make space for each child's pace, and build trust through consistent, patient actions.” This keeps the commitment within your control rather than placing an emotional burden on a child. Our guide to dating someone with children covers family pacing and boundaries separately.

Second-marriage vows after loss

A person who is widowed can honor continuing love and memory without making the new marriage compete with the previous relationship. A simple line is: “Our histories are part of us. I promise to honor yours while choosing the days we have ahead.” Use language that fits the actual couple; there is no one correct emotional script for grief. See dating after widowhood for a different, more focused question.

Funny Wedding Vows That Still Sound Like Real Promises

Funny wedding vows work best when the joke reveals something warm about the couple and is followed by a sincere commitment. Make yourself the punchline more often than your partner; avoid embarrassing details, inside jokes that require explanation, and promises you cannot keep.

“Sam, I promise not to pretend I know the way when the map says otherwise. I promise to be on your side when the dishwasher becomes a debate, to share the last slice when I can, and to tell you honestly when I have eaten it already. More seriously, I promise to take your concerns seriously, even when they arrive at the end of a long day. I will celebrate your wins without making them about me. I will keep making room for fun, and when I get something wrong I will come back with more than an apology. I choose you—especially on the ordinary Tuesdays.”

How much humor belongs in personal vows?

A helpful editing trick: write the serious version first. Add one light detail that you both recognize, then read it aloud. If the vows sound like a comedy routine with commitments attached afterward, remove a joke. If the humor makes your partner feel seen and safe, it may belong.

Promises to avoid in wedding vows

“I will never let you down,” “I will always make you happy,” and “We will never argue” sound grand but cannot be guaranteed. A promise should name behavior within your control. Someone else's happiness, health or reaction is not entirely yours to determine. This is especially important if one partner has been pressured to prove love by agreeing to something they don't want.

Wedding Vow Writing Worksheet: 10 Prompts That Make the Words Your Own

To fill out a wedding vow worksheet, record one vivid moment, one quality you admire, what that quality means in daily life, three realistic commitments, and one honest repair action. You do not need to answer every question. Choose the details that are true, understandable and safe to share publicly.

  1. When did I first notice a quality in my partner that still matters to me?
  2. What ordinary scene—on a bus, in a kitchen, on a walk—captures us better than a perfect vacation?
  3. What do I admire that my partner might not realize I notice?
  4. When life is difficult, what does my partner actually do that makes it feel possible to continue?
  5. What have I learned about caring for someone without trying to control them?
  6. What small, repeatable action could I honestly promise for our everyday life?
  7. What will I choose to do when we disagree or misunderstand each other?
  8. What personal dream of my partner's do I want to support without taking it over?
  9. If I fail to keep a promise, what would sincere repair look like in actions?
  10. What future do we want to build while accepting that life cannot be scripted?

Three-minute writing exercise: set a timer and answer question 2 without editing. Circle the detail your partner would recognize. Underline one action you can promise. That gives you the backbone of a first draft.

Seven Wedding Vow Writing Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid wedding vows that sound borrowed, expose private stories, turn your partner into a joke, make impossible promises, or forget to include an actual commitment. Your partner should recognize themselves in the words and feel respected while hearing them.

  1. Opening with clichés before you know what you mean. Replace “You complete me” with an experience that shows what you appreciate.
  2. Writing an entire biography. One honest detail is more memorable than a chronological tour of every trip and anniversary.
  3. Making your partner responsible for your emotional stability. “I'll never be happy without you” can sound romantic but place unfair pressure on someone else.
  4. Promising perfection. Replace “never fight” with a plan for respect and repair.
  5. Sharing an embarrassing secret. Ask whether stories involving friends, children, former partners, health or trauma are truly yours to tell.
  6. Ignoring the other person's ceremony needs. Agree in advance about approximate length, public humor, private content, and whether you will share drafts.
  7. Forgetting the legal or practical side. The personal vows you write may not be the formal declarations your officiant is required to obtain.

Be careful with copied song lyrics, excerpts from books and popular wedding poetry: beyond sounding impersonal, reproducing lengthy copyrighted text can create rights issues. This guide uses original examples and short, general template language you can adapt.

How to Say Your Wedding Vows Out Loud Without Sounding Rehearsed

Read your wedding vows aloud at least twice, mark places to breathe, and print them in a large, easy-to-read font. Rehearse at a realistic pace rather than racing to hit a time target. Keep a written copy, look up when you can, and allow a pause without apologizing for being emotional.

Practice once while standing, once while holding the page you plan to use, and once with the actual microphone if possible. Long sentences that look elegant on screen can be hard to say after tears or laughter. Shorten them, rather than pushing through. If reading aloud is inaccessible or uncomfortable, work with your officiant on alternatives such as a partner reading, an accessible communication device, a private exchange, or a signed or written presentation.

Should the couple write wedding vows together?

You can write them independently while agreeing on length and tone, collaborate on shared promises, exchange private drafts, or let the vows be a surprise. What matters is consent and expectations, not an internet rule about spontaneity. If one person prefers a short private exchange, a public performance should not be compulsory.

When should you start writing your vows?

Begin with notes early enough to revise without a deadline panic. Professionals interviewed by Vogue suggest giving yourself meaningful brainstorming time; some recommend starting months ahead. You can start today with a single remembered moment even if the ceremony is close.

Are Personal Wedding Vows Legally Binding? Civil Ceremony Rules by Country

Personal wedding vows are not automatically the legal wording required to marry. Some jurisdictions require specific declarations or a registrar-led exchange; optional personal vows may be permitted separately or subject to approval. Before drafting the ceremony order, ask the officiant or registry office what must be said and what can be added.

This article is about writing meaningful personal vows, not legal advice or an alternative to the formal marriage process. A promise written in our editor is not a marriage contract, a certificate, a valid marriage declaration, or a guarantee that an officiant can read it in every venue.

Wedding Vows: Frequently Asked Questions

The short answer: write about a true shared moment, make promises rooted in actions, and choose a length that fits your ceremony. Non-religious vows, second-marriage vows, funny vows and short vows can all feel personal—provided the wording respects your partner and the ceremony requirements.

How do you start wedding vows?

Begin with your partner's name and one true observation: “I remember when...” or “One thing I admire about you is...” Then connect that moment to a promise. You don't need a grand opening.

What should wedding vows include?

Include a personal detail, why you value your partner, a few specific promises and a closing commitment. A wedding vow should contain actual promises, not only compliments.

How many words are 60-second wedding vows?

At a measured speaking rate of 110–125 words per minute, 60 seconds is roughly 110–125 words before substantial pauses. Read it aloud rather than trusting the estimate alone.

What are good short wedding vows?

A short vow can say what you admire, share one tiny memory, promise everyday care and repair after hard moments, and close with a clear choice. The quality of the detail matters more than length.

How do I write wedding vows for my wife?

Write to the person, not a role. Describe a quality that is unmistakably hers and promise actions that fit the relationship you actually have. A “for her” template is optional, not a rule about gender.

How do I write wedding vows for my husband?

Choose a real shared moment, describe something you admire, and make commitments you can practice. If the person is your husband, the most moving wording still comes from your own voice.

Can wedding vows be non-religious?

Yes. You can write personal vows grounded in values, kindness, friendship and choice. For a legal civil ceremony, ask the registrar or officiant which words must be used and which personal passages are permitted.

What should I say in wedding vows for a second marriage?

Honor your current partner and the histories you each bring. Promise respect, shared effort, and room for family relationships without making public judgments about past marriages or children.

Can I use funny wedding vows?

Yes, if the humor is kind and understandable. One real inside detail can make a vow warm; don't let a joke humiliate your partner or replace the promises.

Can you write your own wedding vows at a courthouse?

It depends on the jurisdiction, ceremony format, and registrar. Some places permit extra non-legal personal vows; others have fixed wording or time restrictions. Always confirm before planning the ceremony.

Should wedding vows be the same length?

They don't have to be identical, but discussing a rough length and tone beforehand helps avoid an awkward surprise. One person may want to read 60 seconds and another 90; mutual agreement matters.

Is the free wedding vow writer private?

The editor on this page generates and edits text in the browser and does not transmit or store your words via its own code. You can copy, print, or download your draft locally. Avoid sharing private stories without your partner's permission.
