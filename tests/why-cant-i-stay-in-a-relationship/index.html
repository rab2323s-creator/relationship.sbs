Why can't I stay in a relationship? What this quiz actually explores

If you keep changing partners or struggle to stay in one relationship, it can be tempting to call yourself “bad at commitment.” That explanation may feel convincing, but it does not tell you what happens before each ending. Are you chasing the intensity of a beginning? Feeling trapped when closeness grows? Finding it difficult to be single? Or choosing to leave relationships that genuinely aren't compatible?

This quiz separates those possibilities rather than awarding a single “commitment problem” score. Each answer is associated with one descriptive context. Your result shows how often each appeared, including close alternatives and the responses that led to your profile. The questions are educational, not a validated psychological measure.

Four patterns behind moving from relationship to relationship

1. The excitement of beginnings

Some people love discovering a new person, feeling pursued and experiencing uncertainty. When everyday routine replaces that excitement, they interpret a normal shift as evidence that the relationship is wrong. Our love at first sight psychology guide explores the difference between an initial spark and what develops over time.

2. Feeling crowded when commitment deepens

For others, a partner's request for honest conversations, shared plans or regular contact prompts anxiety or a wish to retreat. This can overlap with themes explored in the Fear of Intimacy Test and Avoidant Attachment Test, but this quiz does not label anyone with an attachment style.

3. Going quickly into a new relationship after a breakup

Moving quickly can reflect enjoying connection, but it can also feel like a way to manage loneliness or an uncertain sense of self. A new partner is not automatically a harmful “rebound.” What matters is whether the choice is freely made, mutually respectful and based on the individual you are dating rather than only the fear of empty evenings.

4. Leaving because the fit really isn't right

Not every short relationship signals avoidance. You may discover a major values conflict, disrespect, incompatible life plans or unsafe conduct. Ending such a relationship can be an appropriate decision. See our Relationship Compatibility Test for a separate reflection on shared values and needs.

What is serial monogamy—and is it an addiction?

Serial monogamy is a descriptive term for having one exclusive relationship after another, sometimes with little time between them. It is not, by itself, a mental-health diagnosis. Likewise, “relationship hopping” is informal language for frequent transitions, not evidence of a disorder. This quiz does not diagnose relationship addiction and cannot establish whether your behavior is compulsive.

A pattern deserves attention when it repeatedly conflicts with your goals, causes distress, makes it hard to respect agreements or stops you from understanding why you choose partners. But having several relationships, being happily single, practicing consensual non-monogamy, or ending an unsafe relationship should not be treated as a symptom simply because a label exists.

A situation that looks like the same problem but isn't

Imagine two people, both of whom have dated three partners in two years. Rowan tends to leave after four months, right when routines develop and the first rush fades; they often remember the excitement of meeting someone new more clearly than any problem in the current partnership. Sam ends relationships when they discover recurring disrespect around boundaries and honest communication. Their dating histories may look similar from the outside, but the questions that would help them are very different.

Rowan might explore what a more stable phase of love feels like. Sam might benefit from noticing incompatible behavior earlier. Neither is helped by being told they simply cannot stay with one person.

Why do I get bored in relationships after a few months?

Early dating often involves novelty, discovery, uncertainty and focused attention. Later, real life becomes more visible. Interest can settle into familiarity. Boredom may point to neglected shared activities, unrealistic expectations of constant excitement, genuine lack of compatibility, or your broader wellbeing. You cannot determine the cause solely by counting months together.

Before ending a caring relationship because the thrill has changed, ask what exactly feels absent: playfulness, connection, common goals, attraction or respect? The answer matters more than the label “bored.” But you also do not owe someone a relationship just because they are kind.

Fear of commitment vs being with the wrong person

These are not mutually exclusive. You might experience discomfort with vulnerability and discover a real incompatibility. Ask whether the impulse to leave appears across otherwise safe relationships when closeness increases, or whether you can identify concrete ongoing difficulties. Have the two of you discussed those difficulties? Are needs negotiable? Is there a pattern of coercion or disrespect?

If a relationship involves intimidation, threats, pressure or abuse, prioritize safety and suitable support. A quiz score is never a reason to remain where you feel unsafe.

How to stop repeating relationship patterns without forcing yourself to stay

  1. Map the last few endings. Write the concrete moment when your thinking changed, what happened first, and what you did afterward.
  2. Separate feelings from actions. Feeling bored, needing independence or feeling lonely doesn't require an immediate breakup or a new partner.
  3. Identify your real deal-breakers. Values and safety matter more than perfection. Don't redefine every disappointment as incompatibility.
  4. Practice a direct conversation. “I'm feeling less engaged lately and I want to understand why” is clearer than disappearing.
  5. Build a life outside dating. Friends, community, routines and interests can make partnership a choice rather than the only source of emotional security.
  6. Seek support if the pattern causes ongoing distress. A qualified counselor can help explore personal history, relationship expectations and repeated choices without reducing everything to a personality label.

What research can—and cannot—tell us

Research on adult attachment describes how people may respond differently to closeness and uncertainty; it cannot tell whether someone who frequently changes relationships has an attachment disorder. Research on rebound relationships also complicates a simple rule that everyone must wait a fixed number of months after a breakup. Self-report studies and observational findings show patterns across groups, not personal diagnoses or guaranteed predictions.

For context, read Mikulincer and Shaver's work on adult attachment and Brumbaugh and Fraley's research on rebound relationships. Frequently asked questions

Why do I keep jumping from one relationship to another?

You may enjoy beginnings, want reassurance after breakups, feel uncomfortable with deepening commitment, or be responding to real incompatibilities. The pattern becomes clearer by examining repeated situations instead of applying a single label.

Does getting bored quickly mean I don't love my partner?

Not necessarily. Early intensity often changes. Boredom can be related to circumstances or a real relationship issue, but it does not by itself reveal what you feel or what decision is right.

Am I a serial monogamist?

The term describes a history of successive exclusive relationships, sometimes with short gaps. It is not a diagnosis, and the timing alone does not show whether your choices are healthy or unhealthy.

Is being addicted to relationships a real diagnosis?

“Relationship addiction” is not established by this quiz, and serial monogamy is not a clinical diagnosis. If dating behavior feels uncontrollable or causes substantial distress, a qualified professional can explore the issue without assuming a label.

Do I have a fear of commitment?

You might if you repeatedly want to withdraw when relationships become emotionally serious, but the same behavior can reflect incompatible needs or safety concerns. This quiz offers a description, not a clinical determination.

Why can't I be single after a breakup?

Time alone may bring sadness, loneliness or uncertainty. Some people seek new companionship quickly; that isn't automatically harmful. Notice whether you're choosing a person freely or mainly avoiding distress.

Can avoidant attachment make you leave relationships?

Discomfort with dependency may be one factor for some people, but attachment styles cannot be inferred from a pattern of breakups alone. The context and behavior of each partner matter.

When is ending a relationship the healthy choice?

Leaving can be appropriate when values or goals are incompatible, boundaries are repeatedly disrespected, or the situation is unsafe. No quiz should pressure you to stay to improve a score.

Can someone change a pattern of leaving relationships quickly?

People can reflect on their choices, communicate needs and build skills over time. No online test can promise change or guarantee a particular relationship will last.
