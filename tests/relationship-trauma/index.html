Relationship Trauma Test: Is Your Past Relationship Still Affecting You?

A relationship can end while some of its rules keep living in you. You may be with someone safer now and still tense when their tone changes. You may know you are allowed to say no and still feel your body preparing for punishment. You may trust the person in front of you while part of you keeps checking whether the past has returned.

This 15-question relationship trauma test is designed to help you notice whether a past painful, toxic, abusive, coercive, or destabilizing relationship may still be shaping your present reactions.

It does not ask whether you are “damaged.” It asks more useful questions: What makes your alarm system activate? Can you trust your own perception? What happens to your boundaries during conflict? Does closeness still feel dangerous? Are new relationships being asked to answer for old injuries?

What This Relationship Trauma Test Measures

A useful relationship trauma quiz should not reduce everything to one vague score. This test looks at five distinct areas that can remain affected after a harmful or destabilizing relationship.

Hypervigilance & threat sensitivity

Do ordinary changes in tone, silence, facial expression, or environment trigger scanning, bracing, or a sense that something bad is about to happen?

Self-trust & reality confidence

Can you trust your own memory and interpretation, or do you automatically assume you are too sensitive, wrong, dramatic, or in need of outside confirmation?

Conflict & boundary fear

Can you disagree, say no, or express hurt without immediately appeasing, over-explaining, retracting, or preparing for punishment?

Intimacy & protective distance

Can safe closeness feel safe, or does emotional or physical intimacy activate guarding, numbness, withdrawal, or the urge to escape?

Past-to-present carryover

Can you evaluate a new person on current evidence, or do present-day cues quickly acquire the meaning they had in your previous relationship?

How the 15-Question Scoring Works

Each answer contributes 0 to 3 reflection points to an overall impact signal and to one of the five dimensions above. The maximum is 45 points.

The final profile does more than show an overall range. It also identifies your two strongest dimensions and can surface recurring patterns such as body-level alarm, self-doubt, appeasing, guarded closeness, or past-present overlap.

The five result profiles are:

  • Steadier Ground — the past is part of your history without strongly organizing the present.
  • Lingering Triggers — specific cues still open older emotional files.
  • Protective Patterns Active — old safety rules are showing up across several areas.
  • Past Relationship Still Shaping the Present — the carryover has broad reach across current relationship life.
  • High Trauma-Related Impact — your answers suggest substantial ongoing effects that may deserve professional assessment and support.

The number is not a diagnosis. It is a way of organizing the response pattern you selected so the result can be more specific than “high” or “low.”

Why the Questions Are Scenario-Based

People are often poor judges of abstract labels. Someone may say “I trust people” and then discover that a delayed reply causes two hours of checking, interpretation, and panic. Someone may believe they are “good with boundaries” but notice that they become apologetic the moment another person looks disappointed.

That is why these questions focus on moments where relationship patterns become visible: silence, conflict, boundaries, ordinary autonomy, intimacy, reminders of the past, and the difference between calm and danger.

Answer based on what happens first and most often over the last several months. Do not answer as the version of yourself you wish you were, and do not let one unusually bad week define the whole test.

Relationship Trauma Test vs Relationship PTSD Test

People often search for a relationship PTSD test, but that phrase can be misleading. PTSD is a formal clinical diagnosis with specific exposure and symptom criteria. There is no separate diagnosis called “relationship PTSD.”

This page therefore does not imitate a PTSD screener and does not tell you whether you have PTSD. Instead, it explores a broader question: whether a past relationship is still affecting threat sensitivity, self-trust, conflict, intimacy, and present-day relationships.

If symptoms are severe, persistent, or affecting sleep, concentration, work, daily functioning, or relationships, a licensed mental-health professional can assess the full clinical picture.

Relationship Trauma vs Trauma Bonding

These are related but different questions.

  • Trauma Bond Test: focuses on attachment to a person who is also causing harm, including push-pull cycles, intermittent relief, fear, and difficulty stepping away.
  • Relationship Trauma Test: focuses on what may remain in you afterward—hypervigilance, self-doubt, conflict fear, guarded intimacy, and old patterns entering new relationships.

If your main problem is that you still feel powerfully attached to the person who hurt you, start with the trauma-bond test. If the relationship is over—or safer now—but its effects are still shaping your reactions, this test is the better fit.

Question Preview: What You Will Be Asked About

The interactive test uses different wording, but these are the situations it explores:

  1. What silence or a change in tone does to your threat system.
  2. Whether conflict makes you doubt your own memory.
  3. What happens internally when you need to say no.
  4. Whether safe closeness still triggers protection.
  5. How delayed contact in a new relationship connects to the past.
  6. Whether calm feels restful or suspicious.
  7. How much ordinary autonomy still requires explanation.
  8. Whether respectful conflict triggers appeasing or freezing.
  9. How easily you can receive affection without guarding.
  10. Whether current people are quickly assigned old motives.
  11. How strongly reminders pull you out of the present.
  12. Whether disagreement erodes trust in your own perception.
  13. What a healthy pause during conflict feels like.
  14. Whether calm love feels safer—or strangely less real—than intensity.
  15. How much the past relationship still organizes your life today.

How to Use Your Result

Use the result to identify a pattern, not to produce a verdict about yourself or another person.

If hypervigilance is strongest, the useful question may be how your body distinguishes a reminder from a current threat. If self-trust is strongest, recovery may involve rebuilding confidence in memory, judgment, and ordinary autonomy. If conflict and boundaries lead, practice may need to focus on staying present without appeasing. If intimacy leads, closeness itself may still activate protection. If carryover leads, the main task may be separating a new person's evidence from an old person's history.

When a Quiz Is Not Enough

If you are currently dealing with violence, threats, coercive control, stalking, or fear for your safety, prioritize real-world support and safety planning rather than using a quiz result to decide whether the situation is serious.

If the relationship is over but you experience persistent intrusive memories, severe avoidance, panic, hypervigilance, sleep disruption, emotional numbness, or major impairment, professional assessment can help clarify what is happening and which treatment approach fits.

Educational self-reflection only. This test does not diagnose PTSD, complex PTSD, anxiety disorders, depression, or any other mental-health condition.
