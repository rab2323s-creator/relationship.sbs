Should We Get a Dog Together? A Practical Decision, Not Just a Cute Idea

Adopting a dog with your boyfriend, girlfriend or spouse might sound like a joyful next chapter. But the first week of puppy photos is different from the three-hundredth ordinary morning when someone is sick, the rain is falling, and the dog still needs to go out. Should we get a dog together? The answer depends on whether you can meet the animal's ongoing needs without putting all the labor on one person.

This free couples dog quiz is for people considering a shared pet. It is deliberately different from our move-in readiness quiz, which measures general household preparation, and our relationship compatibility test, which explores couple dynamics. Here, the dog's welfare is the priority.

What Should Couples Agree on Before Getting a Dog?

Before getting a dog together, decide who provides daily care, who pays routine and emergency vet bills, where the dog can safely live, and how care continues if your relationship or circumstances change. A dog is a living animal with needs for exercise, company, suitable food, shelter and medical attention—not a reward for reaching a relationship milestone.

Imagine two partners who love the same puppy but work opposite shifts. One expects alternating walks; the other assumes the first person will handle them because they suggested adopting. Neither assumption is malicious, yet the dog may lose out unless the couple makes the plan explicit.

The RSPCA's dog welfare resources cover care and behavior; ASPCA general dog care guidance also emphasizes ongoing veterinary care, exercise and basic needs.

Can You Both Actually Take Care of a Dog Every Day?

Start with a typical week, not a holiday. When does the dog eat? Who handles toilet breaks before early shifts? Who is present when the dog needs company? Where can it safely rest? Dogs differ in how long they can comfortably be left alone, and puppies, older dogs and dogs with medical or behavioral needs may require more attention.

Write a timetable for Monday through Sunday, then introduce disruptions: an unexpected late meeting, a train delay, a sick child, a night out, and a weekend away. If the plan collapses on the first unexpected event, it's not ready.

Ask each other:

“If I was sick tomorrow and you were stuck at work, who would take the dog outside and make sure it was fed? Have we asked that person?”

A dependable plan includes an actual backup, not a vague promise that “somebody will help.”

How to Share Dog Responsibilities Without One Partner Carrying Everything

Feeding, walks and picking up waste are visible. Booking vet appointments, buying preventive treatment, noticing behavioral changes, researching pet insurance, tracking medication and arranging sitters are less visible but equally real.

If one person does all the remembering, resentment can build even when the other partner occasionally takes the dog for a long walk. Set a shared list with a primary owner and backup for each duty. This is a dog-specific form of the mental load in relationships problem.

Review the rota after the first month. Work schedules, health and dog needs can change. Fairness is about reliable care and mutually accepted responsibilities, not forcing two people to do exactly half of every activity.

The True Cost of Getting a Dog Together: Food, Vet Bills and Emergencies

A dog budget is not just the adoption fee. Include local prices for food, routine veterinary appointments, preventive care, grooming where relevant, training, equipment, licensing or registration where applicable, pet sitting or boarding, and possible insurance. Costs differ greatly by location and by the individual dog's health and needs.

An unexpected expensive vet visit is a useful test of whether you have a partnership plan: who can authorize urgent help, where the money will come from, how you'll divide expenses, and what happens when one partner loses income. PDSA's cost-of-pet-ownership resources explain why owners should plan for lifetime expenses and vet costs.

Is Your Home—and Your Future Housing—Dog-Friendly?

Check your actual rental agreement and local legal framework, building or housing rules, household members, allergies and the individual animal's physical needs. Confirm whether the space can be made safe and whether an eventual move would have realistic pet-friendly alternatives. Rules and accommodations differ by jurisdiction, so don't rely on a generic international claim that all landlords allow or prohibit dogs.

A couple considering a shared home should first understand lease obligations and general living arrangements in our moving in together checklist. But a good rent agreement cannot replace dog-specific hazard checks, a place to rest, appropriate activity and regular access to care.

Should We Get a Puppy Together or Adopt an Adult Dog?

A puppy can bring frequent toileting, socialization, chewing, interrupted sleep and repeated training. An adult dog may have its own health, adjustment, exercise or behavioral needs. Neither age category automatically means “easy.”

Discuss the actual dog's temperament, size, activity, breed- or individual-related health requirements, existing pets and your family's capacity. If your partner imagines a high-energy hiking companion and you want a quiet apartment dog, don't adopt first and try to resolve the mismatch afterward.

Consider a reputable rescue organization, shelter or responsible source that can tell you about the individual animal, and ask a qualified veterinarian about health needs. Humane, reward-based training and patient adjustment should be part of the plan.

Who Gets the Dog If You Break Up? Plan Care Before Adoption

A breakup plan is not a prediction that your relationship will fail. It is an attempt to spare an animal uncertainty if two humans stop sharing a life. Decide who would be the primary day-to-day caregiver, who is recorded on adoption and microchip documents, who has the capacity to care for the dog, how expenses and belongings are handled, and how emergency or temporary arrangements would work.

Legal ownership and pet custody vary by location. An informal promise to share a dog “50/50” does not necessarily determine legal rights. Review applicable local laws before relying on a contract, and consider qualified advice if the ownership arrangement is complex. The dog's welfare, stability and continuing access to suitable care should remain central rather than becoming a way to retaliate against an ex.

The Couples Dog Adoption Checklist: 12 Things to Decide First

  1. Which specific dog could suit our energy, home and schedule?
  2. Who handles morning and evening walks on workdays?
  3. What happens when we both work late?
  4. Who manages food, medications, grooming and vet appointments?
  5. How do we divide ordinary pet expenses fairly?
  6. What will we do when urgent veterinary care is costly?
  7. Have we checked applicable housing and registration requirements?
  8. How will we keep the home safe and offer suitable enrichment?
  9. Which humane training approach do we both support?
  10. Who can care for the dog during illness or travel?
  11. Who is listed as owner or emergency contact, and do the records match our plans?
  12. How would we protect the dog's stable care if our relationship changed?

Put real names and dates beside answers. A checklist only helps when the agreements are actually feasible.

Should We Get a Dog Together? Frequently Asked Questions

What does this couples dog quiz measure?

This quiz tests six practical planning dimensions: time, shared tasks, vet costs, pet-friendly housing, individual dog welfare and long-term contingencies. It's not a relationship compatibility test or a veterinary assessment.

Are we ready to get a dog quiz?

You're better prepared if both partners can describe exactly who does daily care, how costs are paid, whether housing allows the dog and who takes over during illness or a separation. Strong feelings alone don't cover these needs.

Should we get a dog or puppy together?

A puppy often needs frequent supervision, house training and predictable routines. An adult rescue dog may have different requirements, not necessarily fewer. Choose an individual dog based on its behavior, health, activity and care needs—not just age or appearance.

Is getting a dog together a good idea for our relationship?

It may bring shared experiences, but it cannot resolve poor communication or unequal division of labor. Treat the dog's welfare as a separate responsibility rather than a test of commitment.

Who should take the dog after a breakup?

Discuss reliable care, records, costs, registration and the individual dog's welfare before adoption. Property and pet-custody rules vary by jurisdiction, and a personal understanding may not settle legal ownership.

How much does getting a dog cost?

Costs vary widely by location, size, health, insurance, training, veterinary treatment, food and care. Make a local budget including emergencies rather than trusting a universal average.

Do both partners need to agree on adopting a dog?

In a shared-care plan, both should freely accept their intended responsibilities. If one person does not want pet caregiving, don't assume affection will turn into consent after adoption.

Can our relationship survive getting a puppy?

No quiz can predict that. Puppy care can create sleep disruption and stress, so agree on daily shifts, house training, budgets and conflict repair in advance.
