Open to Closeness

Your answers suggest that emotional closeness is usually something you can move toward without needing distance to feel safe again. You may still value independence, boundaries, and slow trust, but vulnerability itself does not appear to trigger a strong protective retreat.

Guarded but Connected

Your answers suggest that you do not reject intimacy, but you rarely hand someone emotional access quickly. You may need evidence, consistency, and time before deeper vulnerability feels safe. The important question is whether your guard softens when trust is actually earned.

Closeness–Distance Conflict

Your answers suggest an approach–avoid pattern. Part of you may want deep connection, reassurance, and intimacy while another part becomes uncomfortable once the relationship actually offers them. That can create confusing shifts: warmth followed by silence, disclosure followed by regret, or desire followed by sudden fault-finding.

Protective Distance

Your answers suggest that distance may do more than give you breathing room—it may help you feel emotionally safe and in control. Depending on someone, receiving care, or staying present after vulnerable moments can feel risky enough that privacy and self-reliance become your default protection.

Strong Intimacy-Fear Pattern

Your answers suggest that intimacy frequently activates a strong protective response across more than one area: being known, depending on someone, allowing commitment, staying connected after vulnerability, or tolerating emotionally meaningful physical closeness. This does not mean you are incapable of love. It means closeness may currently carry enough threat that distance feels safer.

How to use your result

Focus on the strongest trigger shown in your personalized result, not only the profile name. A useful result should point toward a specific behavior you can observe and practice—not become a permanent identity.
