Fear of Intimacy Test: Are You Afraid of Emotional Closeness?

You can want love and still feel a strong urge to pull away once someone becomes important. This 18-question fear of intimacy test is designed to help you notice where closeness becomes difficult: being emotionally known, depending on someone, receiving care, keeping connection after vulnerable moments, protecting your independence, or tolerating physically affectionate closeness.

This is not a “Do you hate commitment?” quiz. The questions focus on the moment when intimacy becomes real: after the deep conversation, when you need support, when someone is reliably available, when conflict requires repair, or when affection feels emotionally meaningful.

Important distinction

This is an original relationship.sbs self-reflection test. It is not the 35-item Fear-of-Intimacy Scale (FIS) developed for psychological research, and your result is not a diagnosis or clinical score.

What This Fear of Intimacy Test Measures

Instead of collapsing everything into one “high or low” label, the test looks at five connected areas. Your result shows both your overall pattern and the areas that seem to activate the strongest protective response.

Emotional disclosure

Can you say what actually hurts, scares, matters, or feels shameful—or do you become safer when the conversation stays factual?

Receiving support & dependence

What happens when you need someone? Can you accept care, or does needing another person feel like debt, weakness, or loss of power?

Distance after closeness

Do deep conversations, romantic weekends, repair, or consistent availability create an urge to cool the relationship down?

Autonomy & feeling crowded

Can commitment coexist with independence, or do normal relationship expectations begin to feel like engulfment?

Physically affectionate closeness

When touch is wanted, consensual, and physically comfortable, does emotional importance make affection safer—or more exposing?

Physical intimacy is interpreted cautiously. Pain, health factors, sexual difficulties, trauma responses, sensory issues, low desire, consent, and relationship safety can all affect physical closeness and cannot be distinguished by this quiz.

How to Answer for a More Useful Result

Answer for your usual pattern when the relationship matters, not your best day and not the worst relationship you have ever had. If two options feel true, choose the one that describes what you do first when closeness creates pressure.

  • Think about people you genuinely cared about, not casual connections you did not want.
  • Separate healthy boundaries from automatic emotional cutoff.
  • Do not label a safety response as “fear of intimacy” when the other person is controlling, coercive, dishonest, or unsafe.
  • For physical-affection questions, assume the touch is wanted, consensual, and not painful.

Your 5 Possible Result Profiles

The test uses a graded pattern rather than diagnosing a personality type. Your detailed result also identifies which dimension appears strongest.

  • Open to Closeness: intimacy is usually tolerable without giving up boundaries or independence.
  • Guarded but Connected: you open slowly, but evidence of safety can move the wall.
  • Closeness–Distance Conflict: part of you wants connection while another part retreats once you have it.
  • Protective Distance: independence, privacy, or emotional distance may be doing significant safety work.
  • Strong Intimacy-Fear Pattern: closeness activates protection across several areas of connection.

No result means “incapable of love.” The test describes how your answers organize around closeness today.

Fear of Intimacy vs Avoidant Attachment

These ideas overlap but are not identical. Attachment avoidance is a broader relationship pattern involving discomfort with dependence, emotional proximity, and relying on others. Fear of intimacy focuses more specifically on anxiety or defensive distancing around vulnerability and close connection.

Someone can also need a lot of solitude without fearing intimacy. The useful distinction is whether space supports connection—or repeatedly replaces it whenever the relationship becomes emotionally important.

Why This Test Does Not Copy the Fear-of-Intimacy Scale

The best-known research measure is the Fear-of-Intimacy Scale developed by Carol Descutner and Mark Thelen in 1991. It contains 35 items and was designed as a research instrument. Later studies examined its reliability and validity in dating couples and across cultures.

Our test is deliberately different: the questions are original, scenario-based, and designed for educational self-reflection. The scoring combines an overall closeness-protection signal with five practical dimensions. That means the result should be read as a structured reflection—not as an FIS score, diagnosis, or clinical assessment.

What to Do After the Test

The most useful part of the result is not the label. It is the strongest trigger. If disclosure is highest, work on small honest statements. If dependence is highest, experiment with receiving support. If distance-after-closeness leads, focus on staying connected after vulnerable moments. If autonomy is highest, practice explicit boundaries instead of disappearing. If physical closeness is highest, slow down and consider the wider medical, psychological, consent, and relationship context.
