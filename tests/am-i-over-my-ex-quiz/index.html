She was fine until the pharmacy receipt

Mira had survived the obvious things. She stopped rereading the last messages. She returned the spare key. She learned which streets to take when she did not want to pass the café where they had once spent rainy afternoons inventing a future neither of them had been able to keep.

Six months later, she found a receipt in her coat pocket. It was for cough medicine. He had been ill that week; she remembered calling three pharmacies before she found the one open late. For a moment, the ordinary paper seemed heavier than the end of the relationship itself.

She sat on the edge of the bed and thought: Does this mean I am not over him? Then her phone lit up. It was not him. The relief and disappointment arrived so close together she could not separate them.

The next morning she went to work, told a friend a terrible joke, and signed up for a weekend drawing class. No revelation occurred. She still missed him sometimes. But the world had begun to make room for her again.

Am I over my ex? What moving on actually means

Being over an ex does not mean forgetting their face, deleting every photograph, or feeling nothing when an old song comes on. A meaningful relationship may remain part of your history after you no longer wish to restart it.

The more useful question is whether the past relationship continues to organise your present life. Can you make choices that do not depend on what your ex thinks? Can you remember the good without rewriting the reasons it ended? When a reminder hurts, can you eventually return to yourself?

Short answer: You may be moving on even if you still miss your ex. Look at how much your attention, habits, decisions and imagined future depend on that person—not whether you feel perfectly indifferent.

If the breakup is recent, grief can be intense. If you share children, work or another practical responsibility, you might have contact without wanting to reconcile. Neither situation can be judged accurately by a single question about texts or social media.

What the Am I Over My Ex quiz measures

Our original self-reflection quiz looks at four patterns. Each is evaluated separately before the four are combined into a descriptive score:

  • Emotional independence: how you respond to memories, reminders and unfinished feelings.
  • Contact and digital boundaries: whether checking, messaging and interpreting updates remain under your control.
  • A life of your own: your routines, friendships, ambitions and sense of identity apart from the old relationship.
  • Openness to the future: whether new chapters feel possible without a reunion or perfect closure.

The result is not a percentage of how healed you are and not a prediction about your ex. It is a transparent summary of the answers you chose today. You may score differently in a month, even if the relationship itself has not changed.

Signs you are moving on—even when you still feel sad

There is an important difference between remembering someone and organising your future around them. You might see a photo, feel the ache of loss, and continue with the day you had planned. You might wish them well without needing to learn whom they are dating. You might celebrate something good without imagining how it would look to them.

These are not stages everyone passes through in the same order. Recovery is uneven: an anniversary, a song or an unexpected message may sting after weeks of feeling steady. That does not mean you are back at the beginning.

Why checking your ex's social media can make moving on harder

After a breakup, technology can turn absence into a stream of tiny reminders. A Story view, a new follower or a photograph becomes evidence to interpret. Yet the same photo can mean a hundred different things, and most of them are not about you.

In a study of 464 people, more Facebook surveillance of former partners was associated with greater breakup distress, longing and lower reported personal growth. This was observational research, so it does not prove that checking causes distress for every person. A later set of four studies also linked observing ex-partners online with more difficult recovery-related outcomes. Read the earlier study and the later research.

Muting an account, disabling memory notifications or stepping away from a checking ritual can be practical choices. They are not acts of hostility. If you must stay in touch to co-parent or deal with logistics, clear limits can still protect emotional space without requiring complete no contact.

The overlooked part of heartbreak: who you are without the relationship

Mira was not only missing a person. She was missing the version of herself who knew what Thursdays meant, who expected a second toothbrush beside hers, who had learned his family's birthdays by heart. When that shared future vanished, some of her everyday identity vanished with it.

Research on romantic breakups has described changes in self-concept clarity—the ability to see yourself as a coherent person—as one pathway associated with distress. This is a reason to rebuild ordinary life, not a reason to rush into a new relationship. See research on breakup and self-concept.

Try the small, almost unimpressive things: make a meal you love, arrange a Saturday with someone who knew you before the relationship, or choose a routine that has no hidden audience. Progress may look less like a dramatic goodbye and more like a Tuesday you finally enjoy again.

What to do after you get your moving-on quiz result

If your emotional score is lowest

Allow the feeling without turning it into a verdict. Write down both what you miss and what was not working. A balanced memory is often more useful than forcing yourself to dislike someone you once loved.

If your contact score is lowest

Examine the loop: trigger, checking, temporary relief, new uncertainty. Change one part of it. Stop notifications for a week, ask mutual friends not to supply updates, or decide that practical contact belongs in one predictable channel.

If your sense of self is lowest

Recover a small piece of your own calendar. You do not have to reinvent your personality. Start with one commitment that would still matter if your ex never heard about it.

If your future score is lowest

Make a plan that is not a reaction to your breakup. It need not involve dating. A class, trip, savings goal or repaired friendship can make tomorrow feel real without promising that you will never miss the past.

If you are dating again, consider whether you can give a new person attention beyond relief from breakup pain. Our guide to signs someone may be a rebound discusses that situation from the new partner’s perspective—it is not a diagnosis of your own intentions.

If the breakup has severely disrupted sleep, work, safety or functioning for an extended time, a qualified mental-health professional or trusted support person can offer more individualized help than a quiz can.

Am I over my ex? Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I am really over my ex?

Look at repeated patterns: whether you can make plans independently, avoid compulsive checking, accept the ending and imagine a meaningful future. A sad reminder does not necessarily mean you still want to reunite.

Can I be over my ex and still love them?

Yes. Affection, gratitude or tenderness for someone from your past can remain even if you accept that the relationship ended and choose not to return to it.

Why do I still think about my ex every day?

Habit, grief, unresolved questions and shared routines can keep a person mentally present. Frequency alone is less telling than the impact those thoughts have on your decisions and wellbeing.

Does checking my ex's social media mean I am not over them?

Not necessarily. Curiosity happens. Repeated checking that worsens distress or shapes your day may signal an unhelpful habit worth examining.

How long does it take to get over an ex?

There is no universal deadline. Relationship history, loss, circumstances, support and personal meaning affect recovery. This quiz cannot predict a timeline.

Should I date again if I still miss my ex?

Missing an ex does not automatically mean you cannot date. Consider whether you are interested in another person as an individual, rather than hoping they will erase painful feelings.

Is this a scientifically validated breakup recovery test?

No. This is an original educational self-reflection quiz informed by relevant research themes. Its questions, weights and result ranges have not been clinically or psychometrically validated.
