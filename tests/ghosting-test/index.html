Ghosting Test: Are You Likely to Ghost Someone?

This 15-question ghosting test looks at what happens when replying feels awkward, closeness gets real, or you know a connection needs an ending.

It separates ordinary late replies from slow fading, communication overload, conflict avoidance, retreat after closeness, and repeated disappearing.

The message was not hard to answer on Tuesday. By Friday, it felt impossible.

Mara had liked him. That was the confusing part.

The first few weeks were easy: jokes, voice notes, plans that formed without negotiation. Then he asked if she wanted to spend Saturday together. Nothing dramatic. No ultimatum. No pressure.

She saw the message at lunch and thought, I’ll answer when I know how I feel.

That evening, the reply felt late. The next morning, it needed an apology. By Sunday, it seemed to require an explanation for the apology and an answer about Saturday that no longer existed.

So she did the thing that made no sense from the outside and perfect sense for about twelve minutes from the inside.

She answered nothing.

The silence removed the immediate pressure. Then it created a bigger pressure: now she had to explain the silence too.

By the time he sent, “I’m guessing this is your answer,” Mara felt two things at once—relief and guilt.

Ghosting often looks like one behavior from the outside—silence—but the psychology underneath that silence can be very different. One person is avoiding rejection. Another is overloaded. Another retreats when closeness creates expectation. Another simply stops treating the other person’s need for clarity as important.

What Does This Ghosting Test Measure?

This is not a test of whether you have ever forgotten to text back. It looks for a repeated pattern in which silence replaces a decision, a boundary, a rejection, or a repair.

Direct closure tolerance

Can you say “I don’t want to continue” without turning the other person into the manager of your discomfort?

Communication overload

Do unread messages and delayed replies snowball until answering feels harder than disappearing?

Retreat after closeness

Does interest become heavier once another person is emotionally important, available, or clearly invested?

Relational accountability

When you are done internally, does the other person’s need for a legible ending still matter to you?

Why Do I Ghost People?

There is no single “ghosting personality.” Research describes ghosting as a relationship-ending strategy, but the reasons for using it vary. Some people choose it because direct rejection feels uncomfortable. Some experience communication overload. Some find digital connections easier to treat as low-investment. Others withdraw when intimacy or expectation increases.

That is why this test does not turn a high score into an attachment diagnosis. Avoidant attachment can overlap with distancing, but ghosting behavior is not the same thing as having an avoidant attachment style. If closeness itself is the main trigger, compare your result with the Avoidant Attachment Test.

The Delay → Guilt → Avoidance Loop

One of the most realistic paths into ghosting is not a conscious decision to hurt someone. It is a delayed response that becomes emotionally expensive.

  1. You postpone a reply because you are busy, uncertain, or overloaded.
  2. The delay creates guilt.
  3. Guilt makes the reply feel like it now requires an apology or explanation.
  4. The larger task creates more avoidance.
  5. Eventually silence becomes the ending, even if you never explicitly chose it.

If that pattern describes you, the highest-value intervention is often surprisingly small: send an imperfect reply before the delayed message turns into a moral event in your head.

Ghosting vs Avoidant Attachment

They overlap, but they answer different questions.

  • Ghosting Test: What do you do when communication, closeness, or an ending becomes uncomfortable?
  • Avoidant Attachment Test: How do closeness, dependence, and emotional vulnerability affect your attachment behavior more broadly?
  • Am I Emotionally Unavailable? Test: Can you access feelings, receive support, stay present with someone else’s emotion, and return to repair?
  • Conflict Style Test: What happens to your communication strategy once disagreement begins?

Is Ghosting Always Wrong?

No. A stranger on the internet is not entitled to unlimited access to you, and a person who frightens, coerces, stalks, threatens, or repeatedly violates boundaries does not earn a final conversation merely because “closure is polite.”

The useful distinction is between using silence for safety and using silence to escape ordinary interpersonal discomfort. This test deliberately scores those situations differently.

How to Stop Ghosting Without Over-Explaining

You do not need to write an essay every time you want to end a connection. Directness and brevity can coexist.

  • If you are not interested: “I enjoyed meeting you, but I don’t feel the match I’m looking for. I wanted to be clear rather than disappear.”
  • If you are overloaded: “I’m overloaded and slow to reply right now. I’m not ignoring you; I’ll get back to this by Friday.”
  • If you need space: “I need a little space, but I don’t want that to look like disappearing. I’ll reach out after the weekend.”
  • If you are uncertain: “I’m not sure what I want here yet, and I don’t want silence to make the decision for me.”

For more direct wording, use Communication Needs and Healthy Boundary Scripts.

Ghosting Test FAQ

Am I ghosting someone if I reply late?

Not necessarily. Ghosting generally involves withdrawing or ending contact without explanation. A delayed reply that eventually acknowledges the conversation is different from using silence as the ending.

Why do I ghost people I actually like?

For some people, importance increases pressure. The more meaningful the person becomes, the more replying can feel like expectation, vulnerability, or responsibility. That can create a closeness-retreat pattern even when interest is genuine.

Is ghosting a sign of avoidant attachment?

It can overlap with avoidant strategies, but ghosting alone does not establish an attachment style. Overload, rejection discomfort, low investment, conflict avoidance, safety concerns, and other factors can also contribute.

Is blocking someone ghosting?

Context matters. Blocking a threatening or boundary-violating person for safety is not the same as disappearing from an ordinary relationship because a difficult conversation feels uncomfortable.

Can I stop being a ghoster?

Yes. The behavior is specific enough to change. The first step is identifying the moment silence begins doing the work of a decision, then replacing that silence with the smallest honest message that fits the situation.

Research & Methodology

This is an original educational self-reflection test, not a validated clinical instrument. It is informed by research describing ghosting as unilateral relationship withdrawal through nonresponse, studies of ghosters’ and ghostees’ emotional experiences, work on communication overload, and research on different rejection strategies in online dating.
